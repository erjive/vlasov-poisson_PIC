\section{Initial data close to a steady state}
\label{Sec:FixedL}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

In this section we consider initial data of the form~(\ref{Eq:InitialData}), that is, small perturbations of a self-consistent steady state, whose evolution can be compared with the solutions of the linearized equations and with the predictions of the gain $\lambda(\omega)$. We start with the two families of steady states and with the threshold mass above which they possess an oscillating mode. Then, we analyze the response of the gas below the threshold, where it decays, and above it, where it settles down to an oscillation which is not damped. Finally, we show that at finite amplitude the gas particles near the edge of the steady state are trapped by the oscillating mode, and we estimate the amplitude above which this occurs.


\subsection{Steady states}
\label{SubSec:FixedLStates}

We consider the two families of self-consistent steady states of section~\ref{SubSec:SteadyStatesNum}, in both cases with $L_0 = 2$. The first one consists of the lowered Maxwellians~(\ref{Eq:LoweredMaxwellian}) with $W_0 = 3$ in the isochrone potential, with $g = 2$ and $J_{max} = 0.138$ unless otherwise stated and $0.003\leq M_{gas}/M\leq 0.5$. The second one consists of the polytropes~(\ref{Eq:Polytrope}) in the Kepler potential with $k = 0.75$, $1$, $1.25$, $1.5$, $2$ and $3$, $J_{max} = 0.7$ and $0.01\leq M_{gas}/M\leq 1$, for which the dichotomy of Ref.~\cite{mHgRmScS25} has been established. (In the Kepler case the value of $L_0$ can be fixed by means of the rescaling~(\ref{Eq:Rescaling}), such that this family is characterized by $k$, $J_{max}/L_0$ and $M_{gas}/M$.) The two families differ in the width of their frequency bands: in the limit $M_{gas}/M\to 0$ the band is $[0.0602,0.0711]$ for the first one, whose width is only $17$ percent of its mean value, and $[0.0508,0.125]$ for the second one. The properties of the steady states are summarized in Table~\ref{Tab:SteadyStates}. As the mass of the gas increases, the gas contracts and the frequencies of all the orbits grow. In all the cases the DF is a decreasing function of the energy and $\lambda(0) < 1$, such that the steady states are linearly stable with respect to spherically symmetric perturbations (see section~\ref{SubSec:Gain}). Furthermore, the second largest eigenvalue of the operator $S(\omega)$ is smaller than $0.26$ up to the edge of the band, such that there is at most one oscillating mode with frequency below the band.

In the remainder of this section the response of the gas to the perturbation~(\ref{Eq:InitialData}) is described by the function $\chi_1(t)$ defined in Eq.~(\ref{Eq:Response}), which is computed with the weight function~(\ref{Eq:BDef}) with $J_1 = \sigma_1 = 0.05$ for the lowered Maxwellians and with $J_1 = 0.35$ and $\sigma_1 = 0.2$ for the polytropes. With these choices $|\chi_1(0)| = 0.138$ in the first case and $0.17\leq |\chi_1(0)|\leq 0.22$ in the second one. The PIC simulations use $N_J\times N_Q = 400\times 25$ and $\Delta r = 0.1$, with $\Delta t = 0.05$ for the lowered Maxwellians and $\Delta t = 0.1$ for the polytropes, except for the polytrope with $k = 1.25$ and $M_{gas}/M = 1$, for which we use the simulations with $N_J\times N_Q = 1280\times 80$ and $\Delta t = 0.05$ of section~\ref{SubSec:ValResponse}.


\subsection{Gain of the self-consistent loop and threshold mass}
\label{SubSec:FixedLGain}

We have computed the gain $\lambda(\omega)$ with the method of section~\ref{SubSec:GainNum} for all the steady states of the previous subsection. We measure the distance of a frequency $\omega < \Omega_{min}$ from the edge of the band in units of the width of the band,
\begin{equation}
\delta := \frac{\Omega_{min} - \omega}{\Omega_{max} - \Omega_{min}},
\label{Eq:deltaDef}
\end{equation}
and we denote by $\delta_d$ the value of this quantity for the frequency $\omega_d$ of an oscillating mode.

Figure~\ref{Fig:Gain}(c) confirms the behavior of the gain at the edge of the band which was derived in section~\ref{SubSec:Edge}: for $k > 1$ it converges to a finite value $\lambda_{edge}$ when $\delta\to 0$, whereas for $k\leq 1$ it grows without bound. In the interval $10^{-8}\leq \delta\leq 10^{-5}$ the local exponent $d\log(\lambda_{edge} - \lambda)/d\log\delta$ differs from $\min\{ k-1,1 \}$ by at most $0.01$ for $k = 1.25$, $1.5$ and $3$, and for $k = 2$ it lies between $0.92$ and $0.95$, which reflects a logarithmic correction; for $k = 0.75$ and $k = 1$ the gain grows like $\delta^{k-1}$ and $|\log\delta|$, respectively. Figures~\ref{Fig:Gain}(a) and (b) show that $\lambda_{edge}$ increases monotonically with the mass of the gas, proportionally to $M_{gas}/M$ for small masses, and reaches the value one at a threshold mass. We have determined this mass by solving the equation $\lambda_{edge} = 1$ with a bracketing root finder, computing a self-consistent steady state for each trial value of the mass. The results are given in Table~\ref{Tab:Threshold}.

The threshold is much smaller for the lowered Maxwellians than for the polytropes, and within the first family it grows with $J_{max}$. Both observations can be attributed to the width of the frequency band: the narrower the band, the smaller the perturbation of the potential which is required in order to keep the orbits in phase. A threshold of this kind was found by Louis~\cite{pL92} for sequences of one-dimensional polytropes and King models. For the polytropes in the Kepler potential, the results of Ref.~\cite{mHgRmScS25} hold when the mass of the gas is sufficiently small, and Table~\ref{Tab:Threshold} shows how small it has to be: for $k = 1.25$, for instance, the perturbations can only be damped as long as $M_{gas}/M < 0.514$. At a fixed mass, the gain is larger when the DF is less regular at the edge of its support; for the polytropes with $M_{gas}/M = 1$, $\lambda_{edge}$ increases from $0.738$ for $k = 3$ to $1.583$ for $k = 1.25$. This is the quantitative counterpart of the observation of Ref.~\cite{mW91} that a sharp edge favors the existence of an oscillating mode.

When $\lambda_{edge} > 1$, the frequency of the mode follows from Eq.~(\ref{Eq:ModeFrequency}). Slightly above the threshold the mode lies just below the band, as for the modes of the one-dimensional slab~\cite{mW91,pL92}, and as the mass increases it separates from the band (see Table~\ref{Tab:Modes} below). For $k\leq 1$ an oscillating mode exists for any mass, but when the mass is small its frequency lies extremely close to $\Omega_{min}$, as anticipated in Eq.~(\ref{Eq:Binding}): for the polytrope with $k = 0.75$ and $M_{gas}/M = 0.01$ we find $\delta_d = 2.0\times 10^{-8}$, and for $k = 1$ and the same mass the extrapolation of the logarithmic law yields $\delta_d\approx 10^{-98}$. Hence, for the steady states with $k\leq 1$ and small mass, to which the existence result of Ref.~\cite{mHgRmScS25} refers, the oscillating mode is present but cannot be resolved in a time evolution: for $k = 0.75$ and $M_{gas}/M = 0.01$ this would require the time $2\pi/(\Omega_{min} - \omega_d)\approx 4\times 10^9$. A similar difficulty in classifying, by means of time evolutions, the steady states which lie close to the threshold is reported in Ref.~\cite{cS24} for isotropic polytropes.


\subsection{Decay of the perturbation below the threshold}
\label{SubSec:FixedLDamped}

We now turn to the time evolution of the perturbation~(\ref{Eq:InitialData}), starting with the steady states for which $\lambda_{edge} < 1$.

Figure~\ref{Fig:Damped}(a) shows the function $|\chi_1(t)|$ for the lowered Maxwellians with $M_{gas}/M = 0.0065$, $0.042$ and $0.075$, for which $\lambda_{edge} = 0.100$, $0.555$ and $0.874$, respectively. Besides the PIC simulations and the solutions of the linearized equations~(\ref{Eq:LinVlasov},\ref{Eq:LinPoisson}), it shows the solutions of Eq.~(\ref{Eq:LinVlasov}) without its third term, which describe the mixing of the perturbation in the fixed potential $\Phi_{eq}$. The self-gravity of the perturbation delays the mixing: the response falls below one percent of its initial value at $t = 1650$, $2650$ and $11300$ for the three masses, compared to $t = 1510$, $1280$ and $1130$ in the fixed potential. (A delay of the phase mixing by self-gravity has also been found in $N$-body simulations of the vertical motion in a galactic disk~\cite{tAtA26}.) For $M_{gas}/M = 0.075$ the response is a damped oscillation over more than two orders of magnitude, which is the analogue of Landau damping discussed at the end of section~\ref{SubSec:Edge}. With the method of section~\ref{SubSec:Fits} we obtain, in the interval $2000\leq t\leq 4000$, $\omega = 0.07021\pm 6\times 10^{-5}$ and $\gamma = (3.4\pm 0.2)\times 10^{-4}$ from the linearized equations, and $\omega = 0.07028\pm 4\times 10^{-6}$ and $\gamma = (3.48\pm 0.06)\times 10^{-4}$ from the PIC simulation. The frequency lies inside the band, at $3$ percent of its width above the lower edge, and for $t\gtrsim 1.4\times 10^4$ the decay becomes slower, which marks the transition to an algebraic tail. For $M_{gas}/M = 0.058$ the frequency lies at $7$ percent of the width of the band above the edge, and the damping rate is three times larger. Hence, as the threshold is approached from below, the frequency of the damped oscillation approaches the edge of the band and its damping rate tends to zero; above the threshold it becomes the oscillating mode of section~\ref{SubSec:FixedLModes}. This is the behavior described by Louis~\cite{pL92} for the one-dimensional slab, and it corresponds to the divergence of the damping time at the threshold which is found for bunched beams~\cite{iKtAeS21}. In the three cases the PIC simulations reproduce the solutions of the linearized equations to within about one percent, as long as the response exceeds the fluctuations of the reference simulation.

Next, we consider the polytropes with $M_{gas}/M = 0.01$, which is the regime to which the results of Ref.~\cite{mHgRmScS25} refer. In this case the gain is small (for $\delta\geq 10^{-3}$ it does not exceed $0.06$), the response is dominated by mixing, and its behavior for large times is determined by the edge of the support of the DF. For the mixing in the fixed potential $\Phi_{eq}$, Eq.~(\ref{Eq:Tail}) applies with $\hat{F}_1 = \mathcal{F}_{0,eq}\, s/2$, which according to Eq.~(\ref{Eq:PolytropeEdge}) corresponds to $g(J_{max}) = \alpha\,\Omega_{min}^k B(J_{max})/2$ in Eq.~(\ref{Eq:EdgeBehavior}), and one obtains
\begin{equation}
|\chi_1(t)|\simeq \frac{C}{t^{k+1}},\qquad
C := \frac{\Gamma(k+1)\,\alpha\,\Omega_{min}^k B(J_{max})}{2\,|\Omega'(J_{max})|^{k+1}\int_0^{J_{max}} \mathcal{F}_{0,eq}(J) B(J)\, dJ},\qquad t\to \infty.
\label{Eq:TailChi}
\end{equation}
We have verified this expression by solving Eq.~(\ref{Eq:LinVlasov}) without its third term: at $t = 4000$ the envelope of $|\chi_1|$ exceeds the right-hand side of Eq.~(\ref{Eq:TailChi}) by $4$ to $6$ percent for the four values of $k$, and the difference decreases approximately like $1/t$, as expected for the next term of the asymptotic expansion.

The self-gravity of the perturbation modifies this behavior. To see how, we expand $\delta\mathcal{F}_0$ and $\delta\Phi(t,r(Q,J))$ in Fourier series with respect to $Q$, with coefficients $\delta\hat{F}_n(t,J)$ and $\phi_n(t,J)$, respectively. Equation~(\ref{Eq:LinVlasov}) then yields $\partial_t\delta\hat{F}_n + in\Omega\,\delta\hat{F}_n = in\,\mathcal{F}_{0,eq}'\phi_n$, whose solution is
\begin{equation}
\delta\hat{F}_n(t,J) = e^{-in\Omega(J)t}\left[ \delta\hat{F}_n(0,J) + in\,\mathcal{F}_{0,eq}'(J)\,\hat{\Phi}_n(t,J) \right],\qquad
\hat{\Phi}_n(t,J) := \int\limits_0^t \phi_n(t',J) e^{in\Omega(J)t'} dt'.
\label{Eq:Duhamel}
\end{equation}
Hence, the perturbation evolves like in a fixed potential, but with an effective initial datum which accumulates the action of the perturbed potential on each orbit. The important point is that $\mathcal{F}_{0,eq}'$ vanishes like $(J_{max} - J)^{k-1}$ at the edge of the support, that is, one power more slowly than the initial perturbation. Suppose that $k > 1$ and that $\hat{\Phi}_1(t,J_{max})$ converges to a limit $\hat{\Phi}_\infty\neq 0$ for $t\to \infty$. Then, the argument which leads to Eq.~(\ref{Eq:Tail}), applied to both terms on the right-hand side of Eq.~(\ref{Eq:Duhamel}), gives
\begin{equation}
\chi_1(t)\simeq \chi_1^{mix}(t)\left[ 1 + 2|\Omega'(J_{max})|\,\hat{\Phi}_\infty\, t \right],
\label{Eq:TailSG}
\end{equation}
where $\chi_1^{mix}$ denotes the asymptotic expression for the mixing in the fixed potential, whose magnitude is given by Eq.~(\ref{Eq:TailChi}). Therefore, for times which are large compared to $t_\times := (2|\Omega'(J_{max})||\hat{\Phi}_\infty|)^{-1}$ the response decays like $t^{-k}$ instead of $t^{-(k+1)}$: the self-gravity of the perturbation reduces the exponent of the algebraic tail by one. In the derivation of Eq.~(\ref{Eq:TailSG}) we have treated $\hat{\Phi}_1(t,J)$ as a regular function of $J$ which is independent of $t$ at late times. This neglects the potential which is generated by the tail itself; hence, the amplitude of the second term is only correct to leading order in $M_{gas}/M$.

The exponent $-k$ is not new as a decay rate. For perturbations which are proportional to $\mathcal{F}_{0,eq}'$, like the second term in Eq.~(\ref{Eq:Duhamel}), the estimates of Ref.~\cite{mHgRmScS24} give the decay rate $t^{-\min\{ 2,k \}}$ for the time derivative of the potential, and in the Laplace representation of Refs.~\cite{jBaOyY11,jByY13} the exponent results from the singularity of the response function at the edge of the band, which contains $\mathcal{F}_{0,eq}'$. What the present analysis adds is that the self-gravity of the perturbation converts the tail $t^{-(k+1)}$ of a perturbation which is proportional to the DF of the steady state into $t^{-k}$, together with the time $t_\times$ at which this occurs. We are not aware of quantitative results on the decay of the solutions of the full linearized system for steady states of this kind; see the remarks in Ref.~\cite{cS24} and, for steady states with a distribution of angular momenta around a point mass, Ref.~\cite{mHmS25}. \EJ{The derivation of Eq.~(\ref{Eq:TailSG}) is formal and should be checked by Olivier. The Laplace representation would also give the amplitude of the tail beyond the leading order in $M_{gas}/M$; this has not been done.}

The solutions of the full linearized equations for $M_{gas}/M = 0.01$ are shown in Fig.~\ref{Fig:Damped}(b). For $k = 1.5$ and $k = 2$ the function $\hat{\Phi}_1(t,J_{max})$, which we compute from the potential along the outermost rows of the lattice, converges for $t\geq 2000$, and the resulting times $t_\times = 3.5\times 10^4$ and $6.0\times 10^4$ exceed the time span of the solutions. Accordingly, the tail follows the law of the mixing in a fixed potential: the exponents which are obtained from a fit of the envelope in the interval $2000\leq t\leq 4000$ are $-2.58$ and $-3.10$, both with and without self-gravity, where the difference with respect to $-(k+1)$ is due to the correction of the order $1/t$. For $k\leq 1$ the function $\hat{\Phi}_1(t,J_{max})$ does not converge, and the second term in Eq.~(\ref{Eq:TailSG}), evaluated with $\hat{\Phi}_1(t,J_{max})$ in place of $\hat{\Phi}_\infty$, grows to $0.75$ at $t = 8000$ for $k = 0.75$. It accounts for the difference with respect to the mixing in a fixed potential: the envelope with self-gravity exceeds the one without it by a factor which grows from $1.06$ at $t = 2000$ to $1.35$ at $t = 8000$ for $k = 0.75$, and to $1.19$ for $k = 1$. This slow growth is the only trace of the oscillating mode which we find for these steady states. Over the time span considered here the perturbation decays for all the values of $k$, approximately like $t^{-(k+1)}$, and the dichotomy between $k\leq 1$ and $k > 1$ cannot be observed. The PIC simulations agree with the solutions of the linearized equations until the response has decreased by about three orders of magnitude (see section~\ref{SubSec:ValResponse}), such that they confirm the decay and the onset of the tail, whereas the exponents rely on the linearized equations.

The change of the exponent is observed for larger masses. For the polytrope with $k = 2$ and $M_{gas}/M = 1$, for which $\lambda_{edge} = 0.820$ and $t_\times = 65$, the response starts with a damped oscillation, for which a fit in the interval $100\leq t\leq 400$ gives $\omega = 0.1668\pm 0.0008$ and $\gamma = (6.4\pm 0.8)\times 10^{-3}$ for the linearized equations and $\omega = 0.1671\pm 0.0005$ and $\gamma = (6.4\pm 0.9)\times 10^{-3}$ for the PIC simulation with $\varepsilon = 0.1$. For $t > 1500$ it follows an algebraic tail which oscillates with the frequency $\Omega_{min}$, see Fig.~\ref{Fig:Damped}(c). The exponent of its envelope is $-2.07$ in the interval $2000\leq t\leq 4000$ and $-2.04$ in $4000\leq t\leq 8000$, that is, the exponent $-k = -2$ of Eq.~(\ref{Eq:TailSG}) and not the exponent $-3$ of the mixing in a fixed potential, whose amplitude~(\ref{Eq:TailChi}) the tail exceeds by a factor of $400$ at $t = 2000$ and of $1500$ at $t = 8000$. As expected for $M_{gas}/M = 1$, Eq.~(\ref{Eq:TailSG}) does not reproduce the amplitude of the tail, which is about $13$ times larger than the one which results from the computed value $|\hat{\Phi}_\infty| = 0.047$. The same change of the exponent is found for the polytrope with $k = 1.5$ and $M_{gas}/M = 0.6$, for which $\lambda_{edge} = 0.759$: the exponent of the envelope is $-1.54$ in the interval $2000\leq t\leq 4000$ and $-1.52$ in $4000\leq t\leq 8000$, compared to $-2.5$ for the mixing in a fixed potential. \EJ{The PIC simulation for this case has $N = 10^4$ and $\varepsilon = 0.1$. It is to be replaced by the simulations with $N = 1.024\times 10^5$ and $\varepsilon = 0.03$ and $0.1$ which have been run on the cluster and whose analysis is pending.}

\subsection{Finite amplitude: trapping of the orbits near the edge}
\label{SubSec:FixedLTrapping}

In section~\ref{SubSec:ValResponse} we found that for the polytrope with $k = 1.25$ and $M_{gas}/M = 1$ the amplitude of the response falls below the one predicted by the linearized equations by an amount which depends on $\varepsilon$ and which is not a numerical artifact. In this subsection we show that this effect is caused by the gas particles near the edge of the steady state, which are trapped by the oscillating mode when its amplitude exceeds a critical value. That the amplitude of an oscillating mode which lies close to the band is limited was observed by Weinberg~\cite{mW91} in $N$-body simulations of the one-dimensional slab, who attributed this limit to the nonlinear broadening of the frequency of the mode, and Louis~\cite{pL92} pointed out that the trapping of resonant particles leads to oscillations of the amplitude. The pendulum model which follows turns these observations into an explicit condition.

Consider the motion of a gas particle under the influence of an oscillating mode with frequency $\omega_d$. In terms of the action-angle variables of the steady state, this motion is governed by the Hamiltonian $H = E(J) + \delta\Phi(t,r(Q,J))$, see section~\ref{SubSec:Linearization}, and the expansion of section~\ref{SubSec:Gain} gives
\begin{equation}
\delta\Phi(t,r(Q,J)) = \varepsilon\sum\limits_{n\in\Integer} \phi_n(J) e^{i(nQ - \omega_d t)} + c.c.,
\label{Eq:ModePotential}
\end{equation}
where $\phi(r)$ refers to the mode which is excited by the perturbation~(\ref{Eq:InitialData}) with $\varepsilon = 1$ in the linear regime. For an orbit whose frequency $\Omega(J)$ is close to $\omega_d$, the term with $n = 1$ in Eq.~(\ref{Eq:ModePotential}) and its complex conjugate vary slowly along the unperturbed motion $Q = Q_0 + \Omega(J)t$, whereas all the other terms oscillate with frequencies which are at least of the order of $\omega_d$ and average out. Keeping only the former, writing $\phi_1(J) = |\phi_1(J)| e^{i\beta}$ and introducing the angle
\begin{equation}
\vartheta := Q - \omega_d t + \beta,
\label{Eq:thetaDef}
\end{equation}
which together with $J$ constitutes a set of canonical variables, one obtains the time-independent Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
K(\vartheta,J) = E(J) - \omega_d J + 2\varepsilon |\phi_1(J)|\cos\vartheta.
\label{Eq:ResonantH}
\end{equation}
Let $J_r$ be the action variable of the resonant orbit, that is, $\Omega(J_r) = \omega_d$. Since $\omega_d < \Omega_{min}$ and $\Omega$ is a decreasing function, this orbit lies outside the support of the steady state, at the distance
\begin{equation}
d := J_r - J_{max}\approx \frac{\Omega_{min} - \omega_d}{|\Omega'(J_{max})|}
\label{Eq:dDef}
\end{equation}
from its edge. Expanding $E(J) - \omega_d J$ about $J = J_r$ to second order and neglecting the variation of $\phi_1$ and $\Omega'$ between $J_{max}$ and $J_r$, Eq.~(\ref{Eq:ResonantH}) yields, up to an additive constant,
\begin{equation}
K(\vartheta,J)\approx -\frac{1}{2}|\Omega'|(J - J_r)^2 + 2\varepsilon |\phi_1|\cos\vartheta,
\label{Eq:Pendulum}
\end{equation}
which is the Hamiltonian of a pendulum. Its level sets are separated into two classes by the separatrix
\begin{equation}
J = J_r\pm \Delta J_s\left| \cos\frac{\vartheta}{2} \right|,\qquad
\Delta J_s := \frac{2\omega_b}{|\Omega'|},\qquad
\omega_b := \sqrt{2\varepsilon |\phi_1| |\Omega'|}.
\label{Eq:Separatrix}
\end{equation}
A particle which lies inside the separatrix is trapped by the mode: its angle $\vartheta$ oscillates about zero, and its action variable oscillates about $J_r$ with an amplitude of up to $\Delta J_s$ and with a frequency which is equal to the bounce frequency $\omega_b$ close to the center $(\vartheta,J) = (0,J_r)$ and decreases towards the separatrix. Outside the separatrix $\vartheta$ varies monotonically. The same pendulum describes the particles which are trapped by a wave in a plasma, where it determines the nonlinear evolution of Landau damping~\cite{tO65}, and the orbits near a resonance in a galaxy~\cite{BinneyTremaine-Book}.

Initially, all the gas particles have $J\leq J_{max} < J_r$. Therefore, the effect of the mode on them depends on the ratio
\begin{equation}
s := \frac{\Delta J_s}{d} = \frac{2\omega_b}{\Omega_{min} - \omega_d}
\label{Eq:sDef}
\end{equation}
between the half-width of the separatrix and the distance of the resonance from the edge. If $s < 1$, the separatrix does not reach the support of the steady state, and none of the gas particles is trapped. Their action variables oscillate, and it follows from Eq.~(\ref{Eq:Pendulum}) that the largest value which is attained by a particle with $J = J_{max}$ at $t = 0$ is $J_+ = J_{max} + d\,( 1 - \sqrt{1 - s^2} )$. If $s > 1$, the particles close to the edge whose angle $\vartheta$ lies in a suitable interval about zero are trapped, and those which start next to the separatrix are carried to its top, such that $J_+ = J_r + \Delta J_s = J_{max} + d\,(1 + s)$. Since $\omega_b$ is proportional to $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$, the condition $s > 1$ is equivalent to
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon > \varepsilon_c := \frac{(\Omega_{min} - \omega_d)^2}{8 |\phi_1| |\Omega'|}.
\label{Eq:epsc}
\end{equation}
Hence, the closer the frequency of the mode lies to the edge of the band, the smaller is the amplitude which is required for trapping to occur.

In order to evaluate these expressions we determine $\phi_1$ from the solution of the linearized equations, by means of a least-squares fit of the first harmonic $(1/N_Q)\sum_b \delta\Phi(t,r_{ab}) e^{-iQ_b}$ of the perturbation of the potential along the outermost row $a = N_J$ of the lattice. Since the radius is an even function of $Q$, this quantity is real, and according to Eq.~(\ref{Eq:ModePotential}) it is equal to $2\,\re[\phi_1(J) e^{-i\omega_d t}]$ once the transient has decayed. The results are listed in Table~\ref{Tab:Pendulum}; they change by less than two percent when the fit is repeated in a subsequent interval, except for $k = 1.5$, where the change is seven percent.

We stress that the estimate does not contain any adjustable parameter.

The trapping is accompanied by a loss of amplitude of the mode. In the simulations with $s < 1$ the ratio~(\ref{Eq:kappa}) between the amplitudes of the responses of the PIC code and of the linearized equations remains close to one (its smallest value is $0.96$, see Table~\ref{Tab:TrappingRuns}), whereas for $s > 1$ the amplitude falls below the linear one on the time scale $1/\omega_b$ and passes through a minimum at $\omega_b t_0\approx 4$, see Figs.~\ref{Fig:Trapping}(a) and (b). For the polytrope with $k = 1.25$ this minimum is $0.56$ for $\varepsilon = 0.1$ and $0.89$ for $\varepsilon = 0.03$. For the lowered Maxwellian with $M_{gas}/M = 0.097$ the curves for $\varepsilon = 0.03$, $0.1$ and $1$ coincide with each other to within four percent when they are represented as functions of $\omega_b t_0$, and in the longest simulation ($\varepsilon = 1$) the minimum $|\kappa| = 0.83$ is followed by a maximum of $1.24$ at $\omega_b t_0 = 8.0$, such that the amplitude of the mode oscillates with a period which is about $1.4$ times the bounce period $2\pi/\omega_b$. Such oscillations are characteristic for a wave which interacts with trapped particles~\cite{tO65}; for the one-dimensional slab they were anticipated in Ref.~\cite{pL92}. For $\varepsilon = 0.1$ and $M_{gas}/M = 0.097$ we have verified that $|\kappa|$ does not change when the number of particles is multiplied by four or the time step is halved.

The reason for the loss of amplitude is the following. According to Eq.~(\ref{Eq:ModeEq}), in the linearized theory the DF of the mode is proportional to $\mathcal{F}_{0,eq}'(J)\,\phi_1(J)/[\Omega(J) - \omega_d]$, which is largest for the orbits near the edge, where $\Omega(J) - \omega_d$ is smallest. This expression presupposes that the particles follow the unperturbed orbits. For the trapped particles this is not the case: their contribution to the density of the mode ceases to grow in phase with the potential and oscillates with the bounce period instead. Although these particles carry a very small fraction of the mass of the gas (in the simulation with $k = 1.25$ and $\varepsilon = 0.1$, the particles which reach $J > J_{max} + 0.003$ amount to $1.5\times 10^{-4} M_{gas}$), their weight in the mode is large due to the small denominator. Consistently, at comparable values of $s$ the loss is larger for the polytrope with $k = 1.25$ than for the lowered Maxwellian with $g = 2$, since in the former case the derivative of the DF vanishes more slowly at the edge. This is also in line with the analysis by Louis and Gerhard~\cite{pLoG88} of a radial oscillation of finite amplitude of the isochrone sphere, in which the orbits that are trapped around the stable resonant orbits oscillate against the perturbation and damp it, such that a self-consistent oscillation requires a DF which is depleted at the resonances. However, we do not have a quantitative theory for the depth of the minimum.

Finite amplitude effects are also present below the threshold, where the resonant orbit lies inside the support of the steady state. For the lowered Maxwellian with $M_{gas}/M = 0.075$, whose response in the linear regime is damped with the rate $\gamma = 3.4\times 10^{-4}$, we have performed a simulation with $\varepsilon = 0.84$ up to $t = 17200$. In this case the decay of the envelope of $|\chi_1|$ stops at $t\approx 2700$, when it has decreased to $22$ percent of its initial value. Afterwards the envelope grows to $52$ percent at $t\approx 6500$, decreases to $18$ percent at $t\approx 11500$ and grows again. The period of this oscillation, about $8900$, is comparable to the bounce period $2\pi/\omega_b = 7700$ which results from Eq.~(\ref{Eq:Separatrix}) with the mean amplitude of the potential in the interval $4000\leq t\leq 17200$; the same values are obtained with four times as many particles and with half the time step up to $t = 5000$. This is the saturation of Landau damping by the trapping of the resonant particles which was predicted by O'Neil~\cite{tO65} for a plasma wave: the damping stops once the bounce frequency exceeds the damping rate.

We conclude that the linear regime of an oscillating mode is restricted to amplitudes $\varepsilon\ll \varepsilon_c$. For the modes which emerge at the threshold this is a severe restriction, since $\varepsilon_c$ vanishes quadratically with the distance $\Omega_{min} - \omega_d$ of the mode from the band. In this sense, Eq.~(\ref{Eq:epsc}) gives an explicit expression for the amplitude at which the limitation observed in Ref.~\cite{mW91} sets in. The trapping is controlled by the ratio between the bounce frequency and a difference of orbital frequencies. In section~\ref{Sec:FarFromEq} we find that a ratio of the same kind, between the bounce frequency in the potential well of the gas and the spread of the orbital frequencies, determines the evolution of initial data far from equilibrium. \EJ{The simulations of this subsection for $k = 0.75$ and $k = 1$ have $N = 10^4$ and $\varepsilon = 0.1$, and their analysis is restricted to $t\leq 1000$. They are to be replaced by the simulations with $N = 1.024\times 10^5$ and $\varepsilon = 0.03$ and $0.1$ which have been run on the cluster and whose analysis is pending; according to Table~\ref{Tab:Pendulum}, $\varepsilon = 0.03$ lies below the critical amplitude in both cases.}
