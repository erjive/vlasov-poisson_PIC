\section{Numerical methods}
\label{Sec:Numerics}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

In this section we describe the PIC method which we use to solve the nonlinear Vlasov-Poisson system, and the representation of the initial data by the computational particles.


\subsection{Particle-in-cell method}
\label{SubSec:PIC}

In the PIC method~\cite{BirdsallLangdon-Book,HockneyEastwood-Book} the DF is represented by a finite number $N$ of computational particles, each of which stands for a large number of gas particles. In spherical symmetry a computational particle is a spherical shell, which is characterized by its radius $r_j(t)$, its radial momentum $p_j(t)$, its angular momentum $L_j$ and its mass $m_j$, $j = 1,2,\ldots,N$, the last two quantities being constant in time. This corresponds to the approximation
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}(t,r,p_r,L)\approx \sum\limits_{j=1}^N \frac{m_j}{8\pi^2 L_j}\,\delta(r - r_j(t))\,\delta(p_r - p_j(t))\,\delta(L - L_j)
\label{Eq:PICDF}
\end{equation}
of the DF, where the factor $8\pi^2 L_j$ is due to the volume element~(\ref{Eq:PSVolumeForm}). Introducing Eq.~(\ref{Eq:PICDF}) into the Vlasov equation~(\ref{Eq:VlasovSph}) yields the equations of motion
\begin{equation}
\frac{dr_j}{dt} = p_j,\qquad
\frac{dp_j}{dt} = \frac{L_j^2}{r_j^3} - \frac{\partial\Phi_{ext}}{\partial r}(r_j) - \frac{\partial\Phi_{gas}}{\partial r}(t,r_j)
\label{Eq:EOM}
\end{equation}
for the computational particles; in the reduced model $L_j = L_0$ for all $j$. The first two terms on the right-hand side of the second equation are evaluated analytically, whereas the last one requires the solution of the Poisson equation~(\ref{Eq:PoissonSph}). To this purpose we introduce the uniform grid $R_i := (i - 1/2)\Delta r$, $i = 1,2,\ldots,N_r$, which is staggered with respect to the center, and proceed in four steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] The mass of each computational particle is assigned to its two neighboring grid points with the linear weights of the cloud-in-cell scheme~\cite{BirdsallLangdon-Book,HockneyEastwood-Book}, such that the grid point $R_i$ receives the mass
\begin{equation}
\mu_i := \sum\limits_{j=1}^N m_j W\left( \frac{R_i - r_j}{\Delta r} \right),\qquad
W(y) := \max\{ 1 - |y|,0 \}.
\label{Eq:Deposit}
\end{equation}
\item[(ii)] The density is approximated by the continuous, piecewise linear function $\rho(t,r)$ which assumes the values
\begin{equation}
\rho_i := \frac{\mu_i}{4\pi\Delta r\left( R_i^2 + \Delta r^2/6 \right)}
\label{Eq:rhoGrid}
\end{equation}
at the grid points and which is constant for $0\leq r\leq R_1$. The denominator in Eq.~(\ref{Eq:rhoGrid}) is the integral of the function $4\pi r^2 W((r - R_i)/\Delta r)$ over $r$, which guarantees that the mass of the reconstructed density coincides with the total mass of the computational particles.
\item[(iii)] The mass function $m(t,r)$ and the potential are obtained by integrating the equations $\partial m/\partial r = 4\pi r^2\rho$ and $\partial\Phi_{gas}/\partial r = G m/r^2$ outwards from the center. Since $\rho$ is linear on each interval $[R_{i-1},R_i]$, both integrals are computed in closed form, such that no further discretization error is introduced. The additive constant in $\Phi_{gas}$ is fixed by requiring that $\Phi_{gas} + r\,\partial\Phi_{gas}/\partial r = 0$ at the outermost grid point, which matches the potential to the exterior solution $-G M_{gas}/r$.
\item[(iv)] The potential and its gradient are interpolated from the grid points to the positions of the computational particles with the same weights as in Eq.~(\ref{Eq:Deposit}).
\end{enumerate}
For particles which lie within the distance $\Delta r$ from the center, the weights in steps (i) and (iv) are complemented by those of their mirror images at $-r_j$, in accordance with the regularity of the density at the center, and beyond the outermost grid point the exterior solution is used. Neither of these situations plays a role for the configurations considered in these notes, in which the gas occupies a shell that lies well inside the grid; the computational particles which leave the grid for the widest initial data of section~\ref{Sec:FarFromEq} carry less than $10^{-11}$ of the mass of the gas. For the Kepler potential the code uses the potential of a homogeneous sphere of mass $M$ and unit radius, which coincides with the Kepler potential for $r\geq 1$. In all the simulations of these notes with this potential the gas remains in the region $r > 1.7$, such that the two potentials are equivalent.

Note that the potential which acts on a computational particle includes the contribution of the particle itself. In PIC schemes based on Cartesian coordinates the use of the same weights for the assignment and for the interpolation guarantees that a particle exerts no force on itself~\cite{BirdsallLangdon-Book,HockneyEastwood-Book}. In spherical symmetry this is not the case, and it should not be: a thin shell of mass $m$ and radius $r$ is subject to the mean value of the gravitational fields on its two sides, that is, to the force $-G m/(2r^2)$ per unit mass. This is the self-gravity of the shell; the scheme reproduces it up to a correction of the order $\Delta r/r$, and we do not subtract it. In fact, we have verified that subtracting it reduces the order of convergence with respect to $1/N$ from two to one in the collapse of a cold homogeneous sphere, for which the exact solution is known.

Equations~(\ref{Eq:EOM}) are integrated in time with the fourth-order symplectic method of Yoshida~\cite{hY90}, which is a composition of three steps of the second-order leapfrog method with step sizes $w_1\Delta t$, $w_0\Delta t$ and $w_1\Delta t$, where $w_1 := 1/(2 - 2^{1/3})$ and $w_0 := 1 - 2w_1$. A leapfrog step with step size $h$ consists of the update $p_j\mapsto p_j + h f_j/2$ of the momenta, with $f_j$ the right-hand side of the second equation in~(\ref{Eq:EOM}), followed by the update $r_j\mapsto r_j + h p_j$ of the radii, the computation of the potential for the new radii, and a second update of the momenta with the new values of $f_j$. Hence, the Poisson equation is solved three times per time step. We use a symplectic method because, for the motion in a given potential, it does not introduce a secular drift of the energy and of the action variables of the particles, which would otherwise limit the accuracy of the observables at late times. For the same reason we keep the time step $\Delta t$ fixed.

At selected times we compute the total energy~(\ref{Eq:Egas}),
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_{gas}\approx \sum\limits_{j=1}^N m_j\left[ \frac{p_j^2}{2} + \frac{L_j^2}{2r_j^2} + \Phi_{ext}(r_j) + \frac{1}{2}\Phi_{gas}(t,r_j) \right],
\label{Eq:EgasPIC}
\end{equation}
the potential $\Phi_{gas}$ on the grid, from which $\delta\Phi$ is obtained according to Eq.~(\ref{Eq:deltaPhi}), and the functions
\begin{equation}
h_n(t)\approx \sum\limits_{j=1}^N m_j B(J_j,L_j) e^{-inQ_j},
\label{Eq:hnPIC}
\end{equation}
which follow from Eqs.~(\ref{Eq:hk},\ref{Eq:PICDF}). Here $(Q_j,J_j)$ denote the action-angle variables of the particle $j$ at the time $t$ with respect to the potential specified in section~\ref{SubSec:Observables}. They are computed from $(r_j,p_j)$ with the method described in Appendix~\ref{App:ActionAngle}; for the instantaneous potential, $\Phi_{gas}$ is the potential computed by the PIC code on its grid at the time $t$. In the tests of section~\ref{Sec:Validation} we choose the weight function
\begin{equation}
B(J) = J^2\exp\left[ -\frac{(J - J_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2} \right],
\label{Eq:BDef}
\end{equation}
with parameters $J_1$ and $\sigma_1$ which are specified below. The factor $J^2$ makes the function $B(J)e^{-inQ}$ continuous at the circular orbit for $|n|\leq 4$. For the evolution of the gas cloud in section~\ref{Sec:FarFromEq} we choose $B = 1$, such that $h_n/h_0$ is the average of $e^{-inQ}$ over the mass of the gas.

The simulations of these notes use an implementation of this method for the reduced model. Unless otherwise stated, they use $\Delta r = 0.1$, a grid which extends to $r = 20$, a time step $\Delta t = 0.05$ or $0.1$, and $N = 10^4$ computational particles. \EJ{To be decided: whether the codes and the scripts which reproduce the results are made public, in which case the repository should be cited here.}


\subsection{Initial data}
\label{SubSec:QuietStart}

The initial data considered in these notes are given as functions of the action-angle variables $(Q,J)$ of the external potential $\Phi_0 := \Phi_{ext}$.

If the radii and momenta of the computational particles were drawn at random from the initial DF, the observables would be affected by fluctuations of the order of $N^{-1/2}$. For the numbers of particles used in this work, these fluctuations would exceed the values of $|h_n|/h_0$ which are reached during the mixing of the gas. For this reason we place the computational particles on a regular lattice in the variables $(Q,J)$,
\begin{equation}
J_a := \left( a - \frac{1}{2} \right)\frac{J_{max}}{N_J},\quad a = 1,\ldots,N_J,\qquad
Q_b := \left( b - \frac{1}{2} \right)\frac{2\pi}{N_Q},\quad b = 1,\ldots,N_Q,
\label{Eq:Lattice}
\end{equation}
where $[0,J_{max}]$ is the range of the action variable which is covered by the DF, such that $N = N_J N_Q$. The radii and momenta of the particles are obtained by inverting the map $(r,p_r)\mapsto (Q,J)$ which belongs to $\Phi_0$, which only requires the solution of an equation of Kepler's type (see below), and the particle at the lattice point $(Q_b,J_a)$ is given a mass $m_{ab}$ which is proportional to the value of the initial DF at this point, with a constant of proportionality which is determined by the total mass $M_{gas}$. Since the map $(r,p_r)\mapsto (Q,J)$ is symplectic, all the cells of the lattice have the same volume in phase space. Hence, the sums over the computational particles in Eqs.~(\ref{Eq:EgasPIC},\ref{Eq:hnPIC}) are midpoint rules for the corresponding integrals over phase space, and they are free of the fluctuations of a random sample. In plasma physics, a loading of the particles which avoids these fluctuations is known as a quiet start~\cite{BirdsallLangdon-Book}. The method is only reliable over a finite time span: in the absence of self-gravity the rows of the lattice produce a spurious recurrence of the functions $h_n$, which is quantified in section~\ref{Sec:Validation}, and in its presence the regularity of the lattice is eventually lost due to the discreteness of the particles.

As initial datum we consider the gas cloud
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_0(0,Q,J) = \alpha\exp\left[ -\frac{\sin^2(Q/2)}{\sigma_Q^2} \right] J^2\exp\left( -\frac{J^2}{\sigma_J^2} \right),
\label{Eq:Pulse}
\end{equation}
where $(Q,J)$ are the action-angle variables of the external potential and the constant $\alpha > 0$ is determined by the mass of the gas. For $\sigma_Q\ll 1$ the gas particles are concentrated near the pericenters $Q = 0$ of their orbits, and their action variables are distributed about $J = \sigma_J$, see Fig.~\ref{Fig:Pulse}. The factor $J^2$ makes the DF smooth at the circular orbit $J = 0$, where the angle variable is not defined. In the absence of self-gravity, the mixing of this cloud is the test problem of section~\ref{SubSec:ValFree}, and in section~\ref{Sec:FarFromEq} we analyze how its evolution changes when the mass of the gas is increased. We truncate the DF at $J_{max} = 6\sigma_J$, where it has decayed to about $2\times 10^{-14}$ of its maximum. Unless otherwise stated, $\sigma_Q = \sigma_J = 0.1$ and $N_J\times N_Q = 400\times 25$. For the isochrone and the Kepler potentials the inversion of the map $(r,p_r)\mapsto (Q,J)$ only requires the solution of an equation of Kepler's type (see section~\ref{SubSec:AA}), which we solve with Newton's method. In the Kepler case, for instance,
\begin{equation}
r = a(1 - e\cos\eta),\qquad
p_r = \frac{\sqrt{a}\, e\sin\eta}{r},\qquad
Q = \eta - e\sin\eta,
\label{Eq:KeplerInverse}
\end{equation}
with $a := (J + L_0)^2$ and $e := (1 - L_0^2/a)^{1/2}$. When the self-gravity of the gas is taken into account, the observables are computed with the action-angle variables of the instantaneous potential, see section~\ref{SubSec:Observables}.
