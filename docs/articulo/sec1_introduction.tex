\section{Introduction}
\label{Sec:Introduction}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

Collisionless kinetic gases provide the standard description for systems consisting of a large number of particles which interact with each other only through the mean field they generate. Prominent examples are the stars in a galaxy or in a globular cluster, and the dark matter particles in a galactic halo~\cite{BinneyTremaine-Book}. The state of such a gas at time $t$ is described by its one-particle distribution function (DF) $f(t,x,p)$, a nonnegative function on the one-particle phase space which determines the number of gas particles in a small phase space volume about the point $(x,p)$, and whose evolution is governed by the Vlasov equation.\footnote{The Vlasov equation is also often called the collisionless Boltzmann equation in the literature.} In the Newtonian gravitational case, the Vlasov equation is coupled to Poisson's equation for the gravitational potential generated by the gas, which yields the Vlasov-Poisson system, see Ref.~\cite{gR07} for a review of its mathematical properties. Its general relativistic counterpart is the Vlasov-Einstein system~\cite{hA11}.

It is instructive to compare the dynamics of a self-gravitating collisionless gas with the one of a pressureless fluid (dust), which is obtained in the limit in which the velocity dispersion of the gas vanishes. The gravitational collapse of a spherical dust cloud leads to the formation of a singularity in finite time at which the density diverges, both in the Newtonian theory and in general relativity~\cite{jOhS39}; in the latter case, part of this singularity may even be visible to distant observers~\cite{dC84,nOoS11}. In contrast to this, the velocity dispersion of a kinetic gas prevents the particles from focusing. In fact, the Vlasov-Poisson system admits global classical solutions for general initial data~\cite{kP92,pLbP91,jS91}, while for the spherically symmetric Vlasov-Einstein system global solutions exist for small initial data~\cite{gRaR92} and sufficiently concentrated data collapse to a black hole~\cite{hAmKgR11}. Therefore, for a Newtonian collisionless gas the relevant question is not whether or not a singularity forms, but rather how the gas behaves for large times and, in particular, whether or not it relaxes to a stationary state.

A fundamental mechanism leading to such a relaxation is phase space mixing~\cite{dL62,dL67}. To explain it, consider first a gas whose self-gravity is neglected and which is trapped in the potential well of an external potential. If the one-particle Hamiltonian is integrable, each gas particle is confined to an invariant torus, and in terms of action-angle variables $(Q,J)$ the solution of the Vlasov equation is $f(t,Q,J) = F(Q - \Omega(J)t,J)$, where $F$ describes the initial DF and $\Omega(J)$ denotes the frequencies associated with the torus labeled by $J$. Since these frequencies vary from one torus to the other, the DF is stretched along the angle directions and develops structures on finer and finer scales as time increases. As a consequence, $f$ does not converge pointwise as $t\to\infty$. However, the macroscopic observables which are obtained by integrating $f$ against a test function do converge, and their limit is the one associated with the average of the initial DF over the angle variables, which depends only on integrals of motion. Note that this holds for any initial DF, not only for small perturbations of a steady state. A mathematically precise formulation of this statement and its proof for rotationally symmetric potentials in $d = 1,2,3$ space dimensions satisfying a suitable non-degeneracy condition were given by Rioseco and Sarbach~\cite{pRoS20} (see also Refs.~\cite{sTmHdL86,cM19}). The results in Ref.~\cite{pRoS20} include the case of a spherically symmetric gas configuration in which all the gas particles have the same value of the total angular momentum, which behaves like an effective one-dimensional system, and they explain the relaxation to a virialized state which had been observed in the numerical simulations of Ref.~\cite{pDeJmAeMdN17}. The final state is thus determined by the initial DF through its average over the angle variables; for a gas in an axisymmetric potential with two potential wells along the symmetry axis, in which the motion is integrable, this prediction has recently been compared with $N$-particle simulations in Ref.~\cite{aGPmGdNoS26}. For the analogous phenomenon in the exterior of a black hole we refer the reader to Refs.~\cite{pRoS18,pRoS24}, and for estimates on the rate at which the observables converge to Refs.~\cite{mMpRhV22,sCjL22,mHgRmScS24}.

The generalization to the self-gravitating case, which was left open in Ref.~\cite{pRoS20}, is considerably more involved, since the potential now depends on the DF, such that a change of the latter induces a change of the potential which, in turn, acts back on the gas. In plasma physics, where the particles interact through their mean electric field, the corresponding collective effect has been known since the work of Landau~\cite{lL46}: for a gas of charged particles close to a spatially homogeneous equilibrium the electric field decays in time while the DF undergoes mixing. This phenomenon, known as Landau damping, has been established rigorously at the nonlinear level by Mouhot and Villani~\cite{cMcV11}. The gravitational case differs from the electromagnetic one in two important aspects. First, the interaction is attractive, such that the collective response of the gas tends to reinforce a perturbation instead of screening it. Second, a self-gravitating gas configuration of finite mass does not possess spatially homogeneous equilibria. Instead, its steady states are spatially inhomogeneous, the gas particles are trapped in the potential well, and the frequencies of their radial motion lie in an interval which, in the situations considered in these notes, is bounded and bounded away from zero. Mixing damps a collective oscillation through the gas particles whose frequencies coincide with its frequency. For this reason, a collective oscillation whose frequency lies below this interval is not in resonance with any gas particle and cannot be damped by mixing, such that in the gravitational case Landau damping does not always take place.

Two situations have been analyzed in the gravitational case. The first one concerns small perturbations of a self-consistent steady state. Had\v{z}i\'c, Rein and Straub~\cite{mHgRcS22} (see also Refs.~\cite{Kunze-Book,mK22}) showed that the essential spectrum of the operator governing the linearized dynamics about a large class of spherically symmetric steady states is determined by the radial periods of the gas particles, and, using a version of the Birman-Schwinger principle which was first applied to this problem by Mathur~\cite{sM90}, they derived a criterion for the existence of an eigenvalue below this spectrum, which corresponds to an oscillating mode that is not damped. Related criteria and estimates for the number of such modes have been given in Ref.~\cite{mMpRhV26}. For the reduced system in which all the gas particles have the same magnitude of the angular momentum and move around a central point mass, Had\v{z}i\'c, Rein, Schrecker and Straub~\cite{mHgRmScS25} proved the following dichotomy for steady states of sufficiently small mass whose DF is proportional to $(E_0 - E)^k$ for energies $E$ below the cutoff energy $E_0$: the solutions of the linearized system are damped if $k > 1$, whereas an oscillating mode exists if $1/2 < k\leq 1$. Quantitative decay estimates for the linearized system have been obtained in Refs.~\cite{mHgRmScS24,mHmS25}, and particle-in-cell simulations show that, depending on the steady state, its perturbations either oscillate without damping or decay~\cite{tRgR18,cS24}. Perturbations of steady states in the setting of these notes are analyzed in a companion article.\EJ{Reference to the companion article on perturbations of steady states to be added.} The second situation concerns initial data which are not a perturbation of a steady state of the gas. For a self-gravitating gas around a point mass, Chaturvedi and Luk~\cite{sCjL26} proved that small, spherically symmetric initial data which are supported on bounded orbits, with angular momenta in an interval bounded away from zero, undergo phase space mixing up to a time of the order of $\epsilon^{-1}(\log\epsilon^{-1})^{-2}$, where $\epsilon$ measures the size of the data, while the global existence and the asymptotic behavior of these solutions remain open. Finally, when the initial data are far from any steady state and the self-gravity is strong, the gas undergoes a violent relaxation~\cite{dL67} toward a quasi-stationary state, and the prediction of this state from the initial data is understood only in special cases, see Ref.~\cite{yLrPfRtTfB14} for a review.

In these notes, we study numerically the relaxation through phase space mixing of a spherically symmetric collisionless gas which is trapped in an external central potential, whose self-gravity is taken into account, and whose initial DF is far from any steady state. We consider the reduced model in which all the gas particles have the same magnitude $L_0 > 0$ of the angular momentum, which has a two-dimensional phase space and is the setting considered in Refs.~\cite{pRoS20,mHgRmScS25}. This model goes back to the numerical experiments of H\'enon with spherical shells (see Ref.~\cite{mH73} and references therein) and has been used in the $N$-body simulations of Ref.~\cite{pKbM02}. For the external potential we choose the isochrone potential, for which the action-angle variables are known explicitly, and the potential of a point mass, which is the one considered in Refs.~\cite{mHgRmScS25,sCjL26}. As initial data we consider a gas cloud whose particles are concentrated about one orbital phase, with a smooth distribution of their action variables. In the absence of self-gravity this cloud mixes, and we address three questions: how does the self-gravity of the cloud change this relaxation when its mass is increased, which state does the gas reach, and what decides whether it relaxes. Our analysis is based on a particle-in-cell (PIC) code which solves the nonlinear Vlasov-Poisson system, and on a measure of the mixing in terms of the action-angle variables of the instantaneous potential of the gas.

The main results of these notes can be summarized as follows. First, the self-gravity of the cloud decides between two outcomes: either the gas relaxes through phase space mixing, or a dense core forms at the circular orbit and keeps oscillating radially, such that the potential does not relax within the time span of our simulations. Second, we identify the mechanism which decides between them. By averaging the interaction of the gas over the radial oscillations of nearly circular orbits we derive a reduced model, which we call the epicyclic model (section~\ref{Sec:Epicyclic}). In this model the gas is transported in the plane of the complex amplitudes of the radial oscillations like the vorticity of a two-dimensional fluid, by the differential rotation of the orbits and by a rotation which the gas induces on itself. A core forms when the latter carries gas to the circular orbit, where the differential rotation vanishes, before the cloud is wound up. The model contains a single parameter $\Gamma$, the ratio between the angular velocity of the rotation induced by the gas and the one of the differential rotation at the distance of the cloud from the circular orbit. Without adjusting any parameter, it reproduces the PIC simulations, the frequency of the oscillation of the core and the location of the gas particles which are in resonance with it. Third, $\Gamma$, and not the spread of the orbital frequencies, decides whether a core forms. For five different dependences of the DF on the action variable which vanish at the circular orbit, including a narrow ring and clouds whose support does not reach the circular orbit, in the isochrone and in the Kepler potential, the mass of the core reaches ten percent of the mass of the gas at values of $\Gamma$ between $0.23$ and $0.29$, whereas a parameter built with the spread of the frequencies takes values which differ by a factor ten at these points. PIC simulations whose outcome was predicted in advance confirm this. As a consequence, the mass up to which a cloud relaxes grows like the power $3/2$ of its mean action, that is, with its distance from the circular orbit. In the Kepler potential, which is the setting of Ref.~\cite{sCjL26}, the cloud of our reference shape whose mass is one percent of the central mass relaxes, whereas those with four or eight percent leave a core whose oscillation persists. Fourth, the change between the two outcomes is steep but continuous: within the resolution of our simulations there is no critical value of the mass. Fifth, below this change the gas relaxes more slowly than in the absence of self-gravity, and the potential which it generates approaches a stationary profile while the DF continues to mix, which is the analogue of Landau damping for a gas that is far from any steady state. However, the state which is reached is not the average of the initial DF over the angle variables: already for a cloud whose mass is one percent of the central mass, the fraction of the gas on nearly circular orbits is more than ten times larger than in that average. Sixth, the core requires angular momenta which are equal to within about one percent (section~\ref{Sec:DistributedL}). When the angular momenta are distributed, the dependence of the frequencies on $L$ dephases the parts of the core with different angular momenta: for a cloud with three percent of the central mass, a relative spread of $1.4$ percent removes the core, and the gas mixes. The epicyclic model predicted this before the corresponding simulations were carried out. Finally, on the numerical side, our PIC code with particles on a regular lattice in action-angle variables reproduces the exact solution of the mixing problem in the absence of self-gravity to within $6\times 10^{-9}$ in relative terms, and we show that the observables which measure the mixing have to be computed with the action-angle variables of the total potential, since those of the external potential leave a time-independent residue which could be mistaken for an incomplete mixing.\EJ{Reorganized on 15 November 2026 around the mechanism and $\Gamma$; to be reviewed.}

Particle methods have a long tradition in the numerical study of spherically symmetric collisionless systems, both in the Newtonian case~\cite{tRgR18,cS24} and in the relativistic one, where they have been used to analyze the collapse of star clusters and the critical behavior at the threshold of black hole formation~\cite{sStT85,gRaRjS98,Olabarrieta:2000bn,Olabarrieta:2001wy,hAgR06}; see Refs.~\cite{BirdsallLangdon-Book,HockneyEastwood-Book,lapenta2015kinetic} for introductions to the PIC method, Ref.~\cite{Shalaby:2017yok} for a higher-order implementation in the context of plasma physics, and Ref.~\cite{gRtR03} for a convergence proof of a PIC scheme for the spherically symmetric Vlasov-Einstein system. Measuring the relaxation of the observables with such a method is delicate, because the signal has to be separated from the noise which is due to the finite number of computational particles. In these notes we address this problem by placing the particles on a regular lattice in action-angle variables, such that the sums over the particles are quadrature rules for the corresponding integrals over phase space. A loading of the particles which reduces the initial fluctuations (a quiet start) was already used for systems of spherical shells in Ref.~\cite{mH73}. Since the gas does not relax with respect to a fixed potential, we compute the observables with the action-angle variables of the instantaneous potential.

In section~\ref{Sec:Linear} we review the mixing in the absence of self-gravity. Our numerical methods are described in section~\ref{Sec:Numerics}, which also summarizes their validation; the details of the latter are given in Appendix~\ref{Sec:Validation}. Section~\ref{Sec:FarFromEq} is devoted to the evolution of the gas cloud as a function of its mass, for a fixed shape. In section~\ref{Sec:Rates} we analyze the rate at which the clouds which relax mix and at which their potential relaxes, as a function of their mass and of the dependence of the DF on the action variable. In section~\ref{Sec:Epicyclic} we derive the epicyclic model, identify the mechanism and the parameter $\Gamma$, and test them with clouds of other shapes. In section~\ref{Sec:DistributedL} we consider a gas whose particles have a distribution of angular momenta. Conclusions and an outlook are given in section~\ref{Sec:Conclusions}. Technical details regarding the numerical computation of the action-angle variables are included in Appendix~\ref{App:ActionAngle}.
