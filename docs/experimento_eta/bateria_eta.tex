\title{Self-gravity versus phase mixing at fixed angular momentum\\[0.3em]
       \large Linear theory and the design of a particle-in-cell test battery}
\author{Working notes}
\date{24 September 2026; revised 30 September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study whether, in the reduction $f=F(r,p_r)\,\delta(L-L_0)$ with an isochrone
background, the self-gravity of a component of small mass can sustain an undamped
oscillation, and what happens near the transition between damping and oscillation.
The coupling is measured by the ratio $\eta$ between the self-gravitational shift of
the orbital frequencies and the width of the frequency band. The linear problem is
solved with the linearized solver of the repository. For a lowered Maxwellian (the
Wilson model) with the reference width, the collective mode approaches the lower edge
of the band as $\eta$ increases, and its damping rate falls tenfold between
$\eta=0.45$ and $1$; between $\eta=1.0$ and $1.24$ it leaves the band as an undamped
discrete mode. $\eta$ orders the transition, and changing $L_0$ with the width scaled
accordingly does not move it, but the threshold depends on the width of the support,
the shape of the distribution and the exponent of the vacuum edge. With a sharp edge,
King included, the discrete mode is already present at $\eta=1$, and the edge response
persists at $\eta=0.1$, in the direction of the dichotomy proved by Hadžić and
collaborators. The exact criterion is the gain of the self-consistent loop at the lower edge
of the band, $\lambda_{\rm edge}$: in the 25 equilibria with compact support the mode is discrete if
and only if $\lambda_{\rm edge}>1$, whatever the width, shape or edge, and the root of
$\lambda(\omega)=1$ gives its frequency. With a sharp edge the discrete mode exists at weak
coupling, but so close to the edge that no run can separate it. Near the transition the linear damping rate tends to zero while the
bounce frequency of trapped particles does not, so O'Neil's parameter
$\nu=\omega_b/\gamma$ is large for any finite amplitude and the late-time dynamics is
nonlinear: at $\eta=1$, $\varepsilon\approx0.008$ already gives $\nu=1$. We propose a
battery of 30 particle-in-cell (PIC) runs, about \SI{4.5}{h} with four threads (a
core of 12 in about \SI{2}{h}), organized in the $(\eta,\nu)$ plane, with the linear
solution of each equilibrium as reference. All numbers are computed with the scripts
of the repository, not estimated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Theoretical framework}
\label{sec:teoria}

\subsection{The fixed-angular-momentum model}
\label{sec:modelo}
\label{sec:reduccion}

We use units with $G=1$ in which the background is an isochrone of unit mass and unit
scale $b$, so that masses are measured in units of the background mass and times in
units of $(b^3/GM_{\rm iso})^{1/2}$. The self-gravitating component has mass $a_0$;
$a_0=0.1$ is one tenth of the total mass of the background.

In spherical symmetry the distribution function depends on position and velocity only
through the radius $r$, the radial velocity $p_r$ and the modulus $L$ of the angular
momentum. The code solves the reduction
\[
  f(r,p_r,L) = F(r,p_r)\,\delta(L-L_0),
\]
in which all particles have the same angular momentum $L_0$. The radial motion of each
particle is then one-dimensional, with Hamiltonian
\[
  H = \tfrac12 p_r^2 + \Phi_{\rm ef}(r), \qquad
  \Phi_{\rm ef}(r) = \Phi(r) + \frac{L_0^2}{2r^2},
\]
where $\Phi = \Phi_{\rm iso} + \Phi_{\rm self}$ is the total potential: the isochrone
background, $\Phi_{\rm iso} = -1/(1+\sqrt{1+r^2})$, plus the potential of the
distribution itself. The term $L_0^2/2r^2$ is the centrifugal barrier.

\paragraph{The measure $8\pi^2L_0$.} In spherical symmetry the phase-space volume
element is written in terms of $r$, $p_r$ and $L$. The position contributes
$4\pi r^2dr$. The velocity has a radial component $p_r$ and a tangential component
$v_t$, which can point in any direction of the plane perpendicular to $\hat r$, so
$d^3v=2\pi v_t\,dv_t\,dp_r$. Since $L=rv_t$, $v_t\,dv_t=L\,dL/r^2$, and
\[
  d^3x\,d^3v = 4\pi r^2\,dr\cdot\frac{2\pi L\,dL}{r^2}\,dp_r = 8\pi^2L\,dL\,dr\,dp_r .
\]
The delta function in $L$ removes $dL$ and leaves $8\pi^2L_0\,dr\,dp_r$. The mass of the
component is $M=8\pi^2L_0\iint F\,dr\,dp_r$.

The reduction is not an approximation within the model, but it is an extremely
anisotropic model. Everything below holds for it, and comparisons with isotropic
systems require care. The comparison with Hadžić and collaborators
(Section~\ref{sec:borde}) is not affected: their theorem is formulated in exactly this
reduction, with all particles at the same $|L|$ in the field of a central point mass.

\subsection{Angle--action variables}
\label{sec:aa}
\label{sec:circular}

Since $H$ does not depend on time and the radial motion is bounded between two
turning points $r_-<r_+$, each orbit is a closed curve in the $(r,p_r)$ plane, and
angle--action variables exist. The radial \emph{action} is the area enclosed by the
orbit divided by $2\pi$,
\[
  J = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p_r\,dr
    = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{r_-}^{r_+} \sqrt{2\,(E-\Phi_{\rm ef}(r))}\;dr ,
\]
and the \emph{angle} $Q$ is the phase along the orbit, normalized so that it advances
uniformly from $0$ to $2\pi$ in one radial period. The map $(r,p_r)\to(Q,J)$ is
canonical, so it preserves area: $dr\,dp_r=dQ\,dJ$. Since $H$ depends only on $J$,
Hamilton's equations are $\dot J=0$ and $\dot Q=\partial H/\partial J$:
\[
  \Omega(J) = \frac{dE}{dJ} = \frac{2\pi}{T_r(J)}, \qquad
  T_r = 2\int_{r_-}^{r_+}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{2(E-\Phi_{\rm ef})}} ,
\]
with $T_r$ the radial period. Only the isochrone has a closed form:
\begin{gather*}
  E(J) = -\frac{1}{2\,(J+c)^2}, \qquad
  \Omega(J) = \frac{1}{(J+c)^3}, \qquad
  \frac{d\Omega}{dJ} = -\frac{3}{(J+c)^4},\\
  c = \tfrac12\left(L_0+\sqrt{L_0^2+4}\right).
\end{gather*}
Two consequences are used throughout. The frequency \emph{decreases} with the action:
nearly circular orbits ($J\to0$) are the fastest, and $\Omega(0) = c^{-3}$ is the highest
frequency. And the derivative does not vanish, so each frequency corresponds to a single
action. With self-gravity there is no closed form, and the map is computed numerically
(Section~\ref{sec:metodo_equilibrio}).

\paragraph{The circular orbit and the epicycles.} The orbit with $J=0$ is circular. It
lies at the minimum of $\Phi_{\rm ef}$, at the radius $r_c$ where $\Phi_{\rm ef}'(r_c)=0$, that is,
$\Phi'(r_c)=L_0^2/r_c^3$, and has energy $E_c=\Phi_{\rm ef}(r_c)$. With $L_0=2$ and no
self-gravity, $r_c=5.742$.

\emph{Approximation 1: the epicycle.} Near the minimum, $\Phi_{\rm ef}$ is a parabola and
the radial motion is a harmonic oscillator:
\[
  H \approx E_c + \tfrac12 p_r^2 + \tfrac12\kappa^2(r-r_c)^2, \qquad
  \kappa^2 = \Phi_{\rm ef}''(r_c) = \Phi''(r_c)+\frac{3L_0^2}{r_c^4} .
\]
$\kappa$ is the \emph{epicyclic frequency}. The action of an oscillator is its energy divided
by its frequency, so $E\approx E_c+\kappa J$ and $\Omega(0)=\kappa$. The orbit is
\[
  r - r_c = R_e(J)\cos Q, \qquad p_r = -\kappa R_e(J)\sin Q, \qquad
  R_e(J) = \sqrt{2J/\kappa},
\]
where $R_e$, the epicycle radius, is the radial half-width of the orbit. (In this
notation $Q=0$ is the apocentre. The scripts and the code measure $Q$ from pericentre, so
their angle is this one plus $\pi$; the shift does not affect any of the arguments
below.) For the isochrone with $L_0=2$, $\kappa=\Omega(0)=c^{-3}=0.0711$; Section~\ref{sec:corrimiento}
checks it against the density of the background.

\paragraph{Smooth coordinates near the circular orbit.} With $x=\sqrt\kappa\,(r-r_c)$ and
$y=-p_r/\sqrt\kappa$, the orbit is
\[
  x+iy = \sqrt{2J}\,e^{iQ}, \qquad J=\tfrac12(x^2+y^2):
\]
$\sqrt{2J}$ is a radius and $Q$ a polar angle in the $(x,y)$ plane, and the circular orbit is
the origin. The functions $x$, $y$ and $J$ are smooth in $(r,p_r)$, but $Q$ is not defined at
the origin. A function of the form $a(J)\,e^{\pm iQ}$ is therefore smooth at the circular
orbit only if $a(J)=\sqrt J$ times a smooth function of $J$, because
$\sqrt J\,e^{iQ}\propto x+iy$ is smooth and $e^{iQ}=(x+iy)/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ is not. In
general, $e^{\pm ikQ}$ requires a factor $J^{k/2}$.

\subsection{Equilibria}
\label{sec:equilibrio}
\label{sec:familias}

In angle--action variables, with a Hamiltonian that depends only on $J$, the Vlasov
equation is $\partial_tF+\Omega(J)\,\partial_QF=0$, so any $F$ that depends only on $J$ is
stationary. But $J$ is defined in the total potential, and the potential depends on $F$:
the equilibrium must be \emph{self-consistent}. Its construction is described in
Section~\ref{sec:metodo_equilibrio}.

The equilibrium must depend only on $J$ for two reasons. A distribution that depended on
$Q$ would wind up by phase mixing from $t=0$, and that decay would be indistinguishable
from the response to be measured. And without a well-defined equilibrium there is no
fixed frame in which to measure $\delta\Phi$, and neither the damping rate nor the
frequency of the mode would have a meaning.

\paragraph{Normalization.} We write $F_{\rm eq}=A\,\tilde F(J)$, with $\tilde F$ the shape and $A$
the amplitude. Since $dr\,dp_r=dQ\,dJ$ and $\int dQ=2\pi$, the mass is
\[
  a_0 = 8\pi^2L_0\iint F_{\rm eq}\,dQ\,dJ = 16\pi^3L_0\,A\int\tilde F(J)\,dJ ,
\]
which fixes $A$. If the shape is written in the energy, as for the lowered Maxwellian,
$\tilde F$ as a function of $J$ changes with the potential, and $A$ is recomputed at each
iteration.

\paragraph{Three families.} We consider three shapes, all functions of $J$ (or of $E$)
only. Section~\ref{sec:familia} explains the choice between them and of their
parameters.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The Gaussian}, $\tilde F=J^2e^{-J^2/\sigma_J^2}$, the one of the Landau runs.
Its only action scale is $\sigma_J$: with $u=J/\sigma_J$, $\tilde F=\sigma_J^2\,u^2e^{-u^2}$, and
changing $\sigma_J$ only stretches the curve. It has no edge, so its support is defined by
a threshold: where $\tilde F$ exceeds \SI{1}{\%} of its maximum, which is $e^{-1}$ at $u=1$.
Solving $u^2e^{-u^2}=0.01\,e^{-1}$ gives $u=0.061$ and $u=2.763$, so the support is
$[0.061,\,2.763]\,\sigma_J$ and its width $\Delta J\approx2.70\,\sigma_J$.
\item \textbf{The hollow family}, $\tilde F=J^m(J_t-J)^g$ on $[0,J_t)$ and zero outside
(\texttt{-{}-forma politropo}). It has compact support $[0,J_t]$, vanishes as $J^m$ at the
circular orbit and as $(J_t-J)^g$ at the vacuum edge, and has its maximum at
$J=mJ_t/(m+g)$. We use $m=g=2$, $J^2(J_t-J)^2$, with its maximum at $J_t/2$. With $m=0$ it is
the polytrope in the action.
\item \textbf{The lowered Maxwellian} (\texttt{-{}-forma maxwell}), written in the energy:
\begin{gather*}
  F_{\rm eq} = A\,E_\gamma\!\left(g,\,x\right),\qquad x=\frac{E_t-E}{T}\ \ \text{if }E<E_t,\\
  E_\gamma(g,x) = e^x\,P(g,x) = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{x^{g+n}}{\Gamma(g+n+1)},
\end{gather*}
with $P(g,x)$ the regularized lower incomplete gamma function. $E_\gamma$ has two limits
that explain its use. Near the edge, $x\to0$ and $E_\gamma\approx x^g/\Gamma(g+1)$, so $F$
vanishes as $(E_t-E)^g$, with exponent $g$. Far from the edge, $x\gg g$ and
$E_\gamma\to e^x\propto e^{-E/T}$, a Maxwell--Boltzmann distribution. The King model is
$g=1$, $e^x-1$, and the Wilson model $g=2$, $e^x-1-x$. The parameters are $E_t=E(J_t)$, the
energy of the orbit of action $J_t$, and $T=(E_t-E_c)/W_0$, with $W_0$ the depth of the well
in units of the temperature. $E_t$, $T$ and $A$ are recomputed in the potential of each
iteration, so the support is always $[0,J_t]$ and the mass $a_0$. Since $E\approx E_c+\kappa J$
near the circular orbit, $x\approx W_0\,(1-J/J_t)$ to first order. The series of $E_\gamma$ has
positive terms, so it is evaluated without cancellations, also at the edge.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Equivalent widths.} The edge of the compact families is placed at the upper
end of the support of the Gaussian, $J_t=2.763\,\sigma_J$, so that without self-gravity all
families have the same frequency band. The reference width is $\sigma_J=0.05$, or
$J_t=0.138$.

\subsection{Perturbations}
\label{sec:perturbacion}

The initial condition is the equilibrium plus a perturbation in angle,
\[
  F = F_{\rm eq}(J)\,\big[1+\varepsilon\,s(J)\,\chi(Q)\big].
\]

\paragraph{Angular profile $\chi(Q)$.} It must be periodic with zero mean, so that the
integral of $F$ over $Q$, and with it the mass and the equilibrium, do not change. Two
profiles are used (\texttt{-{}-gq}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{cos}: $\chi=\cos Q$. It excites only $k=1$. In the linear regime each
harmonic evolves independently, so exciting a single one gives the cleanest
measurement. It is used unless stated otherwise.
\item \texttt{suma3}: $\chi=\cos Q+\tfrac12\cos2Q+\tfrac13\cos3Q$. It excites $k=1,2,3$ and is
used for the \emph{common-frequency} test, the signature of a genuine mode
(Section~\ref{sec:protocolo}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Radial factor $s(J)$.} Three factors are used (\texttt{-{}-pert}): $s=1$
(\texttt{plana}), $s=\sqrt{J/J_{\max}}$ (\texttt{suave}) and $s=(J/J_{\max})^{3/2}$
(\texttt{suave3}). The choice is set by regularity at the circular orbit
(Section~\ref{sec:aa}):
\begin{itemize}
\item If $F_{\rm eq}(0)>0$, as for the Maxwellian, the flat perturbation is discontinuous:
$F_{\rm eq}(0)\cos Q=F_{\rm eq}(0)\,x/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ jumps at the origin. With $s=\sqrt{J/J_t}$ it
becomes $\propto x$, which is smooth; with $s=(J/J_t)^{3/2}$ it becomes $\propto Jx$, smooth and
smaller near the circular orbit.
\item If $F_{\rm eq}\propto J^2$ near $J=0$, as for the Gaussian and the hollow family,
$F_{\rm eq}\cos Q\propto J^{3/2}x$ is already continuous, and the flat perturbation suffices.
\end{itemize}
The Maxwellian uses \texttt{suave3}, and the hollow family and the Gaussian use
\texttt{plana}. The tails computed in Section~\ref{sec:res_colas} show why \texttt{suave3}
rather than \texttt{suave}.

\paragraph{Positivity.} Since $s\le1$, $F\ge0$ requires $\varepsilon\,\max|\chi|\le1$:
$\varepsilon\le1$ with \texttt{cos} and $\varepsilon\le0.67$ with \texttt{suma3}, whose maximum is
$\approx1.5$. The generator checks this and aborts if it fails.

\subsection{Observables}
\label{sec:observables}
\label{sec:dphi}
\label{sec:hk}
\label{sec:obslineal}

We write $F=F_{\rm eq}+\delta F$ and $\Phi=\Phi_{\rm eq}+\delta\Phi$.

\paragraph{Potential perturbation.} On the radial grid of the code,
\[
  \delta\Phi(r,t) = \Phi(r,t) - \Phi_{\rm iso}(r) - \Phi_{\rm self,eq}(r),
\]
computed by \texttt{landau\_\allowbreak analisis.py}. It does not depend on any angle--action
map, it is what enters the equations of motion, and it is the quantity whose decay the
theorems address. Its size is summarized by $\|\delta\Phi\|(t)$, the rms value over
$r\in[3,15]$, the region where the component with $L_0=2$ lives. As in the Landau runs,
the part due to the perturbation is isolated by subtracting a run with $\varepsilon=0$ of
the same equilibrium: $[\delta\Phi(\varepsilon)-\delta\Phi(0)]/\varepsilon$.

\paragraph{Harmonics.} The angular harmonics of the distribution, weighted by a test
function of the action, are
\[
  h_k(t) = \frac{\sum_j f_j\,B(J_j)\,e^{-ikQ_j}}{\big[\sum_j f_j\,B(J_j)\big]_{t=0}},
  \qquad
  B(J)=J^2\,e^{-(J-j_1)^2/s_{j1}^2},
\]
with the sum over the particles, $f_j$ their weights and $(Q_j,J_j)$ from the map of the
equilibrium potential (\texttt{landau\_\allowbreak analisis.py}). We record $k=0\ldots4$; $h_0$ is
conserved by construction and serves as normalization. The comparison with linear
theory uses $[h_k(\varepsilon)-h_k(0)]/\varepsilon$, and \texttt{lineal.py} gives its linear
analogue, $\sum\delta F\,B\,e^{-ikQ}/\sum F_{\rm eq}B$.

The test function $B(J)$ is a choice of the code (\texttt{analysish.f90}). Its centre and
width follow the support, $j_1=s_{j1}=J_t/2.763$, the same proportion as in the Landau runs
($0.10$ on a support that ends at $0.276$). They are set in the \texttt{.par} file of each
run, from which \texttt{landau\_\allowbreak analisis.py} and, with \texttt{-{}-j1 -{}-sj1},
\texttt{lineal.py} read them. The factor $J^2$ makes $B$ vanish at the circular orbit; the
consequences are given in Section~\ref{sec:colash}.

The Fortran code also computes an $h_k$ (\texttt{analysish.f90}), with an additional factor
in $Q$, $A(Q)=e^{-\sin^2(Q/2)/s_Q^2}$, and with the map of the \emph{bare} isochrone. The factor
does not convolve the harmonics. The code defines $h_k$ as the projection of $f$ on
harmonic $k$ of the test function $A(Q)B(J)$, $\sum_jf_jB(J_j)\,C_k\,e^{-ikQ_j}$ with
$C_k=\langle A(Q)\cos kQ\rangle$ computed once by quadrature, so each harmonic is multiplied by
the constant $C_k$. (Multiplying each particle by $A(Q_j)e^{-ikQ_j}$ would mix harmonics,
but that is not what \texttt{analysish.f90} does.) With $a_0>0$ the $(Q,J)$ of that map are
not the true ones, so the battery uses the $h_k$ of \texttt{landau\_\allowbreak analisis.py}.

\paragraph{General linear observables.} $\delta\Phi$ and $h_k$ are two cases of the same
construction. Any quantity linear in the perturbation is the integral of $\delta F$ over
phase space, weighted by a fixed function $\beta(Q,J)$ that defines the observable,
\[
  O(t) = \iint dQ\,dJ\;\beta(Q,J)\,\delta F(Q,J,t),
\]
and all that matters of $\beta$ for the $k=1$ harmonic are its angular Fourier coefficients
along each orbit,
\[
  \beta_{\pm1}(J)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dQ\;\beta(Q,J)\,e^{\pm iQ}.
\]
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$\delta\Phi$ at radius $r$.} By Poisson's equation in spherical symmetry,
$\delta\Phi(r)=\int G(r,r')\,\delta\rho(r')\,d^3r'$, with $G(r,r')=-1/\max(r,r')$ the Green's
function of spherical shells: a shell at $r'$ contributes $-1/r'$ inside it and $-1/r$
outside. The density follows from integrating $\delta F$ with the measure of
Section~\ref{sec:reduccion}, and in angle--action variables the weight is the Green's
function evaluated where the particle is: $\beta(Q,J)=8\pi^2L_0\,G\big(r,r(Q,J)\big)$. No test
function is involved.
\item \textbf{$h_1$.} $\beta=B(J)\,e^{-iQ}$ divided by the normalization, so $\beta_1=B$ (up to
the constant) and $\beta_{-1}=0$.
\end{itemize}
By Section~\ref{sec:aa}, a weight that is continuous at the circular orbit has
$\beta_{\pm1}\propto\sqrt J$ times a smooth function. So $\beta_{\pm1}\sim J^{1/2}$ is the generic
case, and $\delta\Phi$ satisfies it (Section~\ref{sec:colasdphi}). A $\beta_1$ that vanishes
faster, like the $J^2$ of $B$, is a choice.

\subsection{The frequency band and phase mixing}
\label{sec:mezcla}
\label{sec:banda}

Without self-gravity each particle rotates in the equilibrium potential at its own
frequency. Self-gravity is measured as a deviation from this free evolution.

\paragraph{The band.} The support of $F_{\rm eq}$ occupies an interval of actions
$[J_{\rm lo},J_{\rm hi}]$ and, through $\Omega(J)$, a band of frequencies. Since $\Omega$
decreases with $J$,
\[
  \Omega_{\min}=\Omega(J_{\rm hi}),\qquad \Omega_{\max}=\Omega(J_{\rm lo}),\qquad
  \Delta\Omega=\Omega_{\max}-\Omega_{\min}.
\]
With compact support the band is exactly $[\Omega(J_t),\Omega(0)]$. It is the set of
frequencies at which some particle can resonate, and in the linearized problem it is
the \emph{essential spectrum} (Section~\ref{sec:antonov}): a mode with frequency inside
the band exchanges energy with the resonant particles and is damped, and one with
frequency outside is not. We also use the mass-weighted mean frequency
\[
  \bar\Omega = \frac{\int F_{\rm eq}(J)\,\Omega(J)\,dJ}{\int F_{\rm eq}(J)\,dJ},
\]
since the mass between $J$ and $J+dJ$ is proportional to $F_{\rm eq}(J)\,dJ$ (the measure is
$dQ\,dJ$).

\emph{Approximation 2: where the support ends.} With compact support there is nothing to
choose. For the Gaussian the support is set by the \SI{1}{\%} threshold of
Section~\ref{sec:equilibrio}. This is a convention: another threshold changes the edges
and, with them, the absolute value of $\eta$, but not the comparisons between cases.

\emph{Approximation 3: the relative width to first order.} If the interval of actions
$\Delta J$ is small compared with $J+c$, a Taylor expansion of $\Omega=(J+c)^{-3}$ gives
\[
  \frac{\Delta\Omega}{\Omega} \;\approx\; \frac{|d\Omega/dJ|\,\Delta J}{\Omega}
  \;=\; \frac{3\,\Delta J}{J+c}.
\]
The width of the band therefore grows with the width of the support in action
($\Delta J\approx2.70\,\sigma_J$, or $J_t$) and decreases with $L_0$, which sets $c$.
Section~\ref{sec:res_bandas} compares this formula with the exact bands.

\paragraph{The mixing time.} Two particles at the ends of the band accumulate a phase
difference $k\,\Delta\Omega\,t$ in harmonic $k$. When this difference reaches $2\pi$, the
phases of the harmonic cover the whole circle. This defines
\[
  \tau_k=\frac{2\pi}{k\,\Delta\Omega},
\]
the time over which phase mixing of harmonic $k$ is complete.

\paragraph{The free solution.}
\label{sec:libre}
Without self-gravity the equation $\partial_tF+\Omega(J)\,\partial_QF=0$ is solved by
characteristics: each point keeps its action and its angle advances uniformly,
\[
  F(Q,J,t) = F\big(Q-\Omega(J)\,t,\;J,\;0\big).
\]
For the perturbation of Section~\ref{sec:perturbacion} with $\chi=\cos Q$,
\[
  \delta F(Q,J,t)=\varepsilon\,F_{\rm eq}(J)\,s(J)\cos\big(Q-\Omega(J)t\big).
\]
Substituting in a linear observable and integrating first over $Q$, with
$\cos x=(e^{ix}+e^{-ix})/2$, only the coefficients $\beta_{\pm1}$ survive:
\[
  O(t) = \pi\varepsilon\int dJ\;F_{\rm eq}(J)\,s(J)\,
  \Big[\beta_1(J)\,e^{-i\Omega(J)t}+\beta_{-1}(J)\,e^{i\Omega(J)t}\Big].
\]
For a real weight $\beta_{-1}=\beta_1^*$ and $O=2\pi\varepsilon\,\mathrm{Re}\int
dJ\,\beta_1F_{\rm eq}s\,e^{-i\Omega t}$. For $h_1$, $\beta_1=B$ and
\[
  h_1(t) = \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\frac{\int dJ\,B\,F_{\rm eq}\,s\,e^{-i\Omega(J)t}}{\int dJ\,B\,F_{\rm eq}} .
\]
All the time dependence is in $e^{-i\Omega(J)t}$; the observable enters only through
$\beta_1(J)$, which says how much each orbit weighs in it.

\paragraph{The frequency distribution.} Since $\Omega(J)$ is monotonic we can change
variables, $dJ=d\Omega/|d\Omega/dJ|$:
\[
  O(t)\;\propto\;\int d\Omega\;w(\Omega)\,e^{-i\Omega t},\qquad
  w(\Omega)=\frac{\beta_1\,F_{\rm eq}\,s}{|d\Omega/dJ|}\Big|_{J(\Omega)} .
\]
$w(\Omega)\,d\Omega$ is the weight, in that observable, of the orbits with frequency between
$\Omega$ and $\Omega+d\Omega$. The observable is the Fourier transform of $w$, and the question of
how it decays becomes the question of how the Fourier transform of $w$ decays.

$O(t)$ is a sum of phasors $e^{-i\Omega t}$, one per orbit, weighted by $w$. At $t=0$ they all
point the same way. Each then rotates at its own rate, the phases spread over a range
$\Delta\Omega\,t$, and when this range greatly exceeds $2\pi$ the phasors of neighbouring
frequencies cancel. By the Riemann--Lebesgue lemma, if $w$ is integrable its transform
tends to zero. This is \emph{phase mixing}, and it occurs without dissipation: the
information is not lost but moves to ever finer scales in $J$.

\paragraph{Regularity of $w$ and the form of the decay.}
\label{sec:regularidad}
The cancellation is complete where $w$ is smooth, because each phasor has nearly equal
neighbours on both sides. What survives comes from the points where $w$ is not smooth.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$w$ smooth and vanishing with all its derivatives at the ends.} Each
integration by parts gains a factor $1/t$, so the transform decays faster than any
power. If $w$ is approximately Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma_\Omega$, its transform
is $|O(t)|\approx|O(0)|\,e^{-\sigma_\Omega^2t^2/2}$: Gaussian decay in time.
\item \textbf{An edge.} If near $\Omega_{\min}$, with $u=\Omega-\Omega_{\min}\ge0$, $w\approx c\,u^\alpha$
and $w=0$ below, the substitution $v=ut$ gives
\[
  \int_0^\infty u^\alpha e^{-iut}\,du = t^{-(\alpha+1)}\int_0^\infty v^\alpha e^{-iv}\,dv
  = \Gamma(\alpha+1)\,e^{-i\pi(\alpha+1)/2}\;t^{-(\alpha+1)},
\]
so the edge contributes a tail $c\,\Gamma(\alpha+1)\,e^{-i\Omega_{\min}t}\,(it)^{-(\alpha+1)}$. It
oscillates at the frequency of the edge and decays as a power. At time $t$ only phasors
separated by more than $\sim1/t$ cancel each other; those within $1/t$ of the edge have no
partner on one side and weigh $\int_0^{1/t}u^\alpha du\propto t^{-(\alpha+1)}$. The sharper the
edge, the slower the tail: a jump ($\alpha=0$) leaves $1/t$.
\item \textbf{An interior point} where $w\sim|\Omega-\Omega_*|^\alpha$, or where $w$ changes form
abruptly, contributes in the same way a tail $t^{-(\alpha+1)}$ at frequency $\Omega_*$.
\end{itemize}
Near any point, $\Omega-\Omega_e\approx\Omega'(J_e)\,(J-J_e)$ with $\Omega'\ne0$, so a power in $J$ is
the same power in $\Omega$, and $|\Omega'|$ enters only the amplitude. (If $\Omega'$ vanished
somewhere, a stationary frequency, there would be a stationary-phase contribution
$\propto t^{-1/2}$; this does not happen in the isochrone.) This gives the rule used
throughout.

\paragraph{Tail rule.} \emph{Each point $J_e$ where the integrand $\beta_1F_{\rm eq}s$ behaves as
$|J-J_e|^\alpha$, whether an edge of the band or an interior point, contributes a tail
$\propto t^{-(\alpha+1)}$ at frequency $\Omega(J_e)$. Where the integrand is smooth the decay is
faster than any power. The point with the smallest $\alpha$ dominates.}

\emph{Approximation 4: $\tau_k$ is a scale, not a law.}
\label{sec:tau}
The actual decay is not exponential with rate $1/\tau_k$; its form is given by the tail
rule. $\tau_k$ gives the order of magnitude of the time at which mixing is complete. For
the Gaussian, $w$ is approximately Gaussian, and the \SI{1}{\%} threshold lies where
$e^{-u^2/2}=0.01$, that is, $3.03\,\sigma$ from the centre, so $\Delta\Omega\approx6.1\,\sigma_\Omega$.
Section~\ref{sec:res_libre} tests this with a free run.

\paragraph{Tails of $\delta\Phi$.}
\label{sec:colasdphi}
For $\delta\Phi$ the weight is $\beta=8\pi^2L_0\,G(r,r(Q,J))$, and the free solution gives
\[
  \delta\Phi(r,t) = 8\pi^2L_0\,\varepsilon\;
  \mathrm{Re}\!\int dJ\;F_{\rm eq}(J)\,s(J)\,C(r,J)\,e^{-i\Omega(J)t},
  \qquad
  C(r,J) = \int dQ\;e^{iQ}\,G\big(r,\,r(Q,J)\big).
\]
$C(r,J)$ is the contribution of the $k=1$ harmonic of orbit $J$ to the potential at $r$.
Three properties, which follow only from the orbits and from Poisson's equation, set the
tail:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Circular orbit.} Near it the orbit is an epicycle,
$r(Q,J)\approx r_c+R_e(J)\cos Q$. Expanding $G$ about $r_c$,
$G(r,r_c+R_e\cos Q)\approx G(r,r_c)+\partial_{r'}G\,R_e\cos Q$, and the $Q$ integral of the
constant term vanishes, so $C\approx\pi\,\partial_{r'}G\,R_e\propto\sqrt J$. The end $J=0$ of the
band ($\Omega_{\max}$) contributes an integrand $\propto F_{\rm eq}\,s\,J^{1/2}$. If $F_{\rm eq}(0)>0$,
as for any monotonic distribution, the tail is $t^{-3/2}$ with $s=1$, $t^{-2}$ with
$s=\sqrt{J/J_t}$ and $t^{-3}$ with $s=(J/J_t)^{3/2}$; if $F_{\rm eq}\propto J^2$, it is $t^{-7/2}$.
\item \textbf{Vacuum edge.} $C$ does not vanish at $J_t$, so if $F_{\rm eq}\propto(J_t-J)^g$ the
tail is $t^{-(g+1)}$, at $\Omega_{\min}$.
\item \textbf{Turning points.} $G(r,r')$ has a kink at $r'=r$: it is $-1/r$ for $r'<r$ and
$-1/r'$ for $r'>r$. If the apocentre $r_+(J)$ of the orbit lies just above $r$, the arc of
the orbit beyond $r$ has a half-width in $Q$ proportional to $\sqrt{r_+-r}$ (near apocentre,
$r_+-r(Q)$ grows as the square of the distance in $Q$), and on it $G$ differs from $-1/r$ by
$\approx(r'-r)/r^2\propto r_+-r$. The contribution is $\propto(r_+-r)^{3/2}$, and since
$r_+-r\propto J-J_*$, $C$ has a singularity $(J-J_*)^{3/2}$: a tail $t^{-5/2}$ at frequency
$\Omega(J_*)$, inside the band, \emph{for any} $F_{\rm eq}$. The same holds at pericentre.
\end{itemize}
None of this depends on a test function: the free part of $\delta\Phi$ decays
algebraically for every distribution, the Gaussian included.

\paragraph{Tails of $h_1$ and of any test function.}
\label{sec:colash}
For $h_1$ the integrand is $B\,F_{\rm eq}\,s$. If $B$ behaves as $J^p$ near $J=0$ and does not
vanish at $J_t$, the tail rule gives, with $F_{\rm eq}\sim J^m$ and $s\sim J^{p_s}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item at $J=0$ ($\Omega_{\max}$), a tail $t^{-(p+m+p_s+1)}$;
\item at the vacuum edge, the same $t^{-(g+1)}$ as $\delta\Phi$;
\item no turning-point tail if $B$ is smooth in $J$, because $h_1$ does not look at a fixed
radius.
\end{itemize}
The exponent $p$ is not entirely free: a weight continuous at the circular orbit
requires $p\ge1/2$. The generic case is $p=1/2$, and $\delta\Phi$ is generic ($C\propto\sqrt J$). The
$B$ of the code has $p=2$, which speeds up the tail of the $\Omega_{\max}$ edge by $3/2$ powers
(Table~\ref{tab:exponentes}).

\subsection{Self-gravity: frequency shift, collective mode and the \texorpdfstring{$\eta$}{eta} criterion}
\label{sec:autogravedad}

Self-gravity does two things. It changes the equilibrium: it deepens the potential and
shifts the orbital frequencies. And it couples the perturbation to itself: $\delta F$
produces a $\delta\Phi$ that acts back on the particles, and this coupling can sustain a
collective mode. The $\eta$ criterion uses the first effect as a measure of the second.

\paragraph{The frequency shift.}
\label{sec:corrimiento}
Self-gravity deepens the potential and speeds up the orbits, so the whole band moves up.
The nearly circular orbit, whose frequency is $\kappa$, shows by how much. Since
$\Phi'(r_c)=L_0^2/r_c^3$, the definition of $\kappa$ can be written
$\kappa^2=\Phi''+3L_0^2/r_c^4=\Phi''+3\Phi'/r_c$. Poisson's equation in spherical symmetry,
$\Phi''+2\Phi'/r=4\pi G\rho$, eliminates $\Phi''$, and with $\Phi'=GM(<r)/r^2$,
\[
  \kappa^2 = 4\pi G\,\rho(r_c) + \frac{G\,M(<r_c)}{r_c^3} .
\]
The radial frequency depends on two things: the enclosed mass, as for Kepler orbits,
and \emph{the local density at the radius of the orbit}. The second is the important one.

For the isochrone with $L_0=2$, $r_c=5.742$. The density of the isochrone of unit mass and
scale is $\rho = [3(1+a)a^2 - r^2(1+3a)]/[4\pi(1+a)^3a^3]$ with $a=\sqrt{1+r^2}$; at $r_c$ it is
$1.09\times10^{-4}$, and the enclosed mass is $0.696$. Then $4\pi\rho = 1.37\times10^{-3}$,
$M/r_c^3 = 3.68\times10^{-3}$ and $\kappa = 0.0711$, equal to $\Omega(0) = c^{-3} = 0.0711$: the
epicyclic approximation is exact in the circular limit, as it must be.

The component of mass $a_0$ lives in a shell around $r_c$ whose half-width is the typical
epicycle radius. For the Gaussian, taking $J=\sigma_J$ (the maximum of
$J^2e^{-J^2/\sigma_J^2}$), $R_e=0.92$, $1.19$ and $1.68$ for $\sigma_J=0.03$, $0.05$ and $0.10$. The
maximum of $J^2(J_t-J)^2$ is at $J_t/2=1.4\,\sigma_J$, which gives a similar shell. The Wilson
Maxwellian, instead, has its maximum at $J=0$, and its mean action is $0.20\,J_t=0.027$ for
$J_t=0.138$: $R_e=0.87$ against $1.19$, a narrower and denser shell.

\emph{Approximation 5: uniform shell.} If the mass is spread uniformly over
$[r_c-R_e,\,r_c+R_e]$, its density is $\rho_{\rm self}\approx a_0/(4\pi r_c^2\,2R_e)$, and half of the mass
lies inside $r_c$. The formula above then gives
\[
  \delta\kappa^2 \approx \frac{a_0}{2R_e\,r_c^2} + \frac{a_0/2}{r_c^3},
  \qquad
  \frac{\delta\kappa}{\kappa} = \frac{\delta\kappa^2}{2\kappa^2}.
\]
Section~\ref{sec:res_corrimiento} compares this estimate with the measured shift.

\paragraph{The linearized equation.}
\label{sec:respuesta}
The Vlasov equation is $\partial_tF+\{F,H\}=0$, with the Poisson bracket in angle--action
variables $\{F,H\}=\partial_QF\,\partial_JH-\partial_JF\,\partial_QH$. With $F=F_{\rm eq}(J)+\delta F$ and
$H=H_0(J)+\delta\Phi(Q,J,t)$, where $H_0$ is the Hamiltonian of the equilibrium and
$\partial_JH_0=\Omega$, the first-order terms are
\[
  \partial_t\,\delta F + \Omega(J)\,\partial_Q\,\delta F
  - F_{\rm eq}'(J)\,\partial_Q\,\delta\Phi = 0 .
\]
The second term is the free transport of Section~\ref{sec:libre}; the third is the field
of the perturbation acting on the equilibrium. $\delta\Phi$ is in turn the potential of
$\delta F$, through Poisson's equation, so the system is closed. \texttt{lineal.py} solves
exactly this system (Section~\ref{sec:metodo_lineal}).

\paragraph{Modes.} For a solution $\propto e^{i(kQ-\omega t)}$ the equation gives
\[
  \delta F_k(J) = \frac{k\,F_{\rm eq}'(J)}{k\,\Omega(J)-\omega}\;\delta\Phi_k(J),
\]
with $\delta\Phi_k(J)$ the coefficient $k$ of the potential along orbit $J$. The denominator
vanishes where $k\Omega(J)=\omega$: these are the \emph{resonant} particles. $\delta F_k$ produces a
density and, through Poisson's equation, a $\delta\Phi$. Requiring it to be the one assumed
gives a self-consistency condition whose solutions are the modes. Schematically,
\[
  \epsilon(\omega) = 1 - (\text{coupling}\propto G\,a_0)\int dJ\;
  \frac{k\,F_{\rm eq}'(J)\,|\Psi(J)|^2}{k\,\Omega(J)-\omega} = 0 ,
\]
with $\Psi$ the radial shape of the mode. In the actual problem the coupling is a matrix
in a basis of radial functions (Kalnajs's method), not a scalar; the scalar form
suffices for the argument.

\paragraph{Where the mode can live.} If $\omega$ lies inside the band, the integral has a
real pole and the root moves into the complex plane with negative imaginary part: the
mode is damped (Landau damping). If $\omega$ lies outside, there is no pole, the root is real
and the mode oscillates without damping. We write the root as $\omega-i\gamma$, so that the mode
behaves as $e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega t+\dots)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\omega$ is the \emph{frequency} of the mode and $\gamma\ge0$ its \emph{damping rate};
\item $x=(\omega-\Omega_{\min})/\Delta\Omega$ is its \emph{position in the band}: $0<x<1$ is inside
(resonant, damped) and $x<0$ below (a discrete mode, $\gamma=0$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The Antonov form.}
\label{sec:antonov}
For radial perturbations of a spherical system there is a more precise form, due to
Antonov: the linearized equation can be written $\partial_t^2\psi=-\mathcal A\psi$ with $\mathcal A$ a
self-adjoint operator. Two of its properties are used here.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Stability.} If $\partial F_{\rm eq}/\partial E<0$ over the whole support (the
distribution is \emph{monotonic}), $\mathcal A$ is positive and $\omega^2>0$: there are no
growing radial modes. An oscillation that does not decay in a monotonic distribution is
a genuine neutral mode, not an instability. In the work of Hadžić and collaborators the
operator is defined in a space with weight $1/|\partial F/\partial E|$, which requires this
monotonicity; this should be confirmed in the section on hypotheses of the reference
paper.
\item \textbf{Where the mode appears.} The essential spectrum of $\mathcal A$ is
$\{k^2\Omega(J)^2\}$, the band of Section~\ref{sec:banda}. The gravitational term of $\mathcal A$ is
\emph{attractive} and tends to lower the eigenvalues, so a discrete mode, if it exists,
appears \emph{below} $\Omega_{\min}^2$, or in a gap between the bands of consecutive $k$. These gaps
exist when $\Omega_{\max}/\Omega_{\min}<2$; with the width of the Landau runs this ratio is $1.37$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The regenerated component at $\Omega_{\max}$.}
\label{sec:regenerada}
\emph{Approximation 6.} The formula for $\delta F_k$ shows what self-gravity generates near
the circular orbit. There $\delta\Phi_1(J)$, the coefficient of the potential along the
orbit, is proportional to $R_e\propto\sqrt J$, by the same argument that gives $C\propto\sqrt J$
(Section~\ref{sec:colasdphi}). Then $\delta F_1\propto F_{\rm eq}'(0)\,\sqrt J$ near $J=0$: self-gravity
generates a component with the regularity of $s=\sqrt{J/J_t}$, with an amplitude
proportional to the coupling and \emph{independent of the initial condition}. Its tail is
$t^{-2}$ at $\Omega_{\max}$ in any generic observable, $\delta\Phi$ included, and $t^{-7/2}$ in $h_1$
with $p=2$ (Table~\ref{tab:exponentes}).

In a monotonic distribution $F_{\rm eq}'(0)\ne0$ (for the Maxwellian,
$F_{\rm eq}'=-F_{\rm eq}\,\Omega/T$ far from the edge), so the component is always present. In a
distribution that vanishes as $J^2$, $F_{\rm eq}'(0)=0$ and it does not appear. This is not a
defect of the distribution but of the fixed-$L$ model: with distributed $L$, $\kappa(L)$
varies and blurs the edge $\Omega_{\max}$. Section~\ref{sec:borde_lineal} confirms the
component.

\paragraph{The $\eta$ criterion.}
\label{sec:eta}
\emph{Approximation 7: the shift as a measure of the coupling.} The shift of the mean
frequency of the equilibrium \emph{is not} the distance travelled by the mode: it is an
effect of the same coupling on the background state. It is used as a measure of the
strength of the coupling because both are of the same order: both come from terms
$\sim4\pi G\rho_{\rm self}$, as the formula for $\kappa^2$ shows. The question of whether the
coupling suffices to pull the mode out of the band then becomes a comparison between
this shift and the width of the band.

The shift is measured by comparing two averages of the same quantity, the mass-weighted
mean frequency of Section~\ref{sec:banda}:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\bar\Omega$, computed in the self-consistent equilibrium, with the mass $a_0$ included
in the potential;
\item $\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}$, the same average for the same distribution of orbits (the same
shape $\tilde F$, with the same $J_t$, $g$ and $W_0$), computed in the isochrone alone, as if
the component had no mass ($a_0\to0$). It is the mean frequency that these orbits would
have without self-gravity.
\end{itemize}
The difference between the two is the effect of self-gravity. The definition is
\[
  \delta\Omega = \frac{\bar\Omega-\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}}{\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}}, \qquad
  \boxed{\;\eta = \frac{\delta\Omega}{\Delta\Omega/\bar\Omega}\;}
\]
$\delta\Omega$ is the fractional increase of the mean frequency due to self-gravity, and
$\Delta\Omega/\bar\Omega$ is the relative width of the band with mass: $\eta$ is the relative shift
divided by the relative width, a dimensionless number. This is how \texttt{eta.py}
computes it, and all values of $\eta$ in this document are defined in this way.

For example, for the Wilson model with $J_t=0.138$ and $a_0=0.075$ (case A4 of
Section~\ref{sec:lineal}), the mean frequency without self-gravity is
$\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}=0.0687$, a mean radial period of $91.4$. With the mass, $\bar\Omega=0.0820$, so
$\delta\Omega=0.193$: self-gravity speeds up the orbits by \SI{19}{\%}. The band with mass is
$[0.0698,\,0.0857]$, its relative width is $\Delta\Omega/\bar\Omega=0.194$, and $\eta=0.193/0.194=1.00$.

$\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}$ is not $\Omega(0)$. $\Omega(0)=\kappa$ is the frequency of a single orbit, the
circular one, the fastest in the band; $\bar\Omega$ and $\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}$ are averages over all
the orbits of the distribution.

$\eta$ is a design criterion with a physical basis, not a theorem. What $\eta$ guarantees is
that the cases explore the range where the transition \emph{can} occur, instead of lying
entirely on one side of it. Section~\ref{sec:barrido} shows that $\eta$ orders the
transition but does not fix it alone. The exact criterion is the following.

\paragraph{The loop gain at the edge.}
\label{sec:lazo}
The density sees only the part of $\delta F$ that is even in $Q$, because $r(-Q)=r(Q)$. With
$b_k=\delta F_k+\delta F_{-k}$, the formula for $\delta F_k$ above gives
\[
  b_k(J)=R_k(J;\omega)\,\delta\Phi_k(J),\qquad
  R_k(J;\omega)=\frac{2k^2\,\Omega(J)\,F_{\rm eq}'(J)}{k^2\Omega(J)^2-\omega^2},\quad k\ge1,
\]
and the potential of the shells closes the loop:
\begin{gather*}
  \delta\Phi_k(J)=\sum_{k'\ge1}\int M_{kk'}(J,J')\,b_{k'}(J')\,dJ',\\
  M_{kk'}(J,J')=4\pi L_0\iint\cos kQ\,\cos k'Q'\,G\big(r(Q,J),r(Q',J')\big)\,dQ\,dQ',
\end{gather*}
with $G(r,r')=-1/\max(r,r')$. This is the matrix form of the response method, with the harmonics in $Q$ as the basis
along the orbits. A mode of frequency $\omega$ exists if $MR(\omega)$, which maps a potential to
the potential of the response it produces, has the eigenvalue $1$. $M$ is negative, since
gravity attracts. With $F_{\rm eq}'<0$ and $\omega<\Omega_{\min}$ so is $R$, and $MR$ is similar to the
symmetric positive operator $|R|^{1/2}(-M)|R|^{1/2}$. Its largest eigenvalue $\lambda(\omega)$ is the
gain of the loop. $\lambda$ grows with $\omega$ up to the edge and decreases along imaginary
frequencies, so a value $\lambda(0)\ge1$ would imply a growing mode, and Antonov's stability
gives $\lambda(0)<1$. Hence there is a discrete mode below the band if and only if
\[
  \lambda_{\rm edge}\equiv\lim_{\omega\to\Omega_{\min}^-}\lambda(\omega)>1,
\]
and its frequency is the root of $\lambda(\omega_d)=1$. In quantum mechanics this is the
Birman--Schwinger criterion for a bound state at the edge of a band. Near the edge
$|R_1|\propto(J_t-J)^{g-1}/(\Omega-\omega)$, so $\lambda_{\rm edge}$ is finite only for $g>1$, and
\[
  \lambda_{\rm edge}-\lambda(\Omega_{\min}-\delta)\propto\delta^{\min(g-1,\,1)},
\]
with a logarithm for $g=2$; for $g\le1$, $\lambda$ diverges at the edge. Inverting this law
gives the distance $\delta_d$ of the mode below the edge. Near the threshold,
$\delta_d\propto(\lambda_{\rm edge}-1)^{1/(g-1)}$ for $1<g<2$, and $\delta_d\propto\lambda_{\rm edge}-1$ up to the logarithm
for $g\ge2$. For $g<1$ there is a mode at any coupling, at a distance that decreases as the
power $1/(1-g)$ of the coupling, and for $g=1$ the distance decreases exponentially. The
threshold $\lambda_{\rm edge}=1$ does not depend on the family; Section~\ref{sec:res_lazo}
computes it for all the equilibria.

\subsection{The vacuum edge and the Hadžić dichotomy}
\label{sec:borde}

Hadžić, Rein, Schrecker and Straub (\emph{Damping versus oscillations for a
gravitational Vlasov--Poisson system}, Arch.\ Ration.\ Mech.\ Anal.\ \textbf{249}, 45, 2025;
\texttt{arXiv:2301.07662}) work in \emph{the same reduction as this document}: the radial
Vlasov--Poisson system with all particles at the same $|L|$, in the fixed potential of a
central point mass $M$, with small equilibria $f=\varepsilon\,(E_0-E)_+^k$ ($\varepsilon$ is a size
parameter, the analogue of our $a_0$). Their Theorem~1.2 states the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $1/2<k\le1$, there is $\varepsilon_0(k)>0$ such that for $0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0$ the
linearized problem is \emph{not} damped: there is at least one eigenvalue below the bottom
of the essential spectrum, in the principal gap, that is, a discrete mode with frequency
below $\Omega_{\min}$.
\item If $k>1$, there is $\varepsilon_0(k)>0$ such that for $0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0$ there is Landau
damping (in a weak sense, without a rate) and the point spectrum is empty.
\end{itemize}
Only the regularity at the vacuum edge matters: the result holds for any $F$ that behaves
as $(E_0-E)^k$ there, and the authors note that the King model ($k=1$) is not damped. The
proof has two parts: the absence of eigenvalues embedded in the essential spectrum, and
a Birman--Schwinger principle for the eigenvalues in the principal gap.

The two differences from the present setting are the background, an isochrone instead
of a point mass (both give a radial period that is monotonic in the energy, which is the
property the proof uses), and the size: the theorem holds for $\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0(k)$ and does
not estimate $\varepsilon_0$. For $k=g>1$ nothing prevents a discrete mode from appearing at
finite mass. The linear calculations of Section~\ref{sec:lineal} estimate that threshold
for $g=2$ and test the case $g\le1$ at small mass. Related work: Ramming and Rein (Phys.\ D
\textbf{365}, 72, 2018) study numerically oscillating solutions of the radial problem
without a central mass; Rioseco and Sarbach (Class.\ Quantum Grav.\ \textbf{37}, 195027,
2020) study phase mixing in an external central potential; and Hadžić, Rein and Straub
(Arch.\ Ration.\ Mech.\ Anal.\ \textbf{243}, 611, 2022) prove the existence of linearly
oscillating galaxies.

The tail rule gives an intuition for why the edge matters. An edge at which the weight
vanishes as $(\Omega-\Omega_{\min})^g$ produces in the free response a tail $\propto t^{-(g+1)}$,
slower for smaller $g$. With self-gravity, a free response that lingers near the edge
gives the coupling more time to feed it back and sustain an oscillation. This is a
heuristic, not the proof.

The edges of the three families are the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The Gaussian} has no edge at $\Omega_{\min}$: it switches off smoothly.
\item \textbf{The hollow family} has a vacuum edge at $J_t$ with exponent $g=2$, and vanishes
as $J^2$ at the circular orbit.
\item \textbf{The Wilson Maxwellian} has a genuine vacuum edge at $J_t$, the low-frequency end
of the band towards which the coupling pushes the mode: $F\propto(E_t-E)^2$, with $g=2$, on
the damped side and away from the marginal case $g=1$, which is King. At $J=0$ it is
maximal, as is every monotonic distribution and as are the equilibria of that work.
\end{itemize}
With the Maxwellian the edge mechanism is not switched off but fixed in a known class,
and the edge experiment consists in changing a single number, $g$, at fixed $\eta$. Since
it is a linear question, it is answered with the linear solver
(Section~\ref{sec:borde_lineal}).

\subsection{Nonlinear scales and the limits of linear theory}
\label{sec:objecion}
\label{sec:atrapamiento}

\paragraph{The perturbative objection.} An initial condition $F=F_{\rm eq}\,[1+\varepsilon\,s\,\chi(Q)]$
suggests a perturbation, and with it a predictable objection: if the problem is linear,
linear theory (Kalnajs's response, or the linearized solver of this repository) solves
it, and the particle simulation adds nothing. Part of the objection is correct, and the
design accepts it. Every question that is linear (whether a discrete mode exists, the
values of $\gamma$ and $\omega$, and how they depend on $\eta$ and on the edge) is answered here
with \texttt{lineal.py}, without particles. This is cheap and precise, and it is the
natural comparison with the theorems of Hadžić and collaborators, which are linear
(Section~\ref{sec:lineal}). The PIC runs in the linear regime serve as validation, not as
results. But the conclusion that the code is unnecessary does not follow, for the
reasons below.

\paragraph{The parameter is not $\varepsilon$.} In a fixed potential the Vlasov equation is
\emph{exactly linear} in $F$: the free solution of Section~\ref{sec:libre} holds for any
$\varepsilon$. Nonlinearity enters only through $\delta\Phi$, which is proportional to the mass,
to the amplitude and to how much potential the shape of the perturbation produces,
$\delta\Phi\propto a_0\,\varepsilon\,\rho_\Phi$, with $\rho_\Phi$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:res_colas}. With
$\varepsilon=1$, $\delta F/F$ is of order one, and the problem can still be linear if the mass is
small; conversely, a small $\varepsilon$ can make it nonlinear, for the reasons that follow.
That the initial condition looks like a perturbation says nothing: what decides is the
size of $\delta\Phi$ compared with two rates.

\paragraph{Two nonlinear scales.} \emph{Approximation 8: the pendulum.} Near a resonance
$k\Omega(J_r)=\omega$, a particle feels the Hamiltonian $H=H_0(J)+|\delta\Phi_k|\cos(kQ-\omega t)$. In
the frame rotating with the wave, with the angle $\psi=kQ-\omega t$ and its conjugate momentum
$I=J/k$, the Hamiltonian is $K=H_0(kI)-\omega I+|\delta\Phi_k|\cos\psi$. Expanding about the
resonance, $H_0(J)\approx H_0(J_r)+\Omega(J_r)(J-J_r)+\tfrac12\Omega'(J_r)(J-J_r)^2$, the linear term
cancels with $-\omega I$, and
\[
  K \approx \tfrac12\,k^2\Omega'(J_r)\,(I-I_r)^2 + |\delta\Phi_k|\cos\psi ,
\]
a pendulum. Its small oscillations have the \emph{bounce frequency}, and its separatrix sets
the half-width of the \emph{trapping island}:
\[
  \omega_b = k\,\sqrt{|\Omega'(J_r)|\,|\delta\Phi_k|}, \qquad
  \Delta J_{\rm atr} = 2\sqrt{|\delta\Phi_k|/|\Omega'(J_r)|}.
\]
Particles inside the island are trapped by the wave and oscillate about its phase. The
approximation assumes an isolated resonance and an amplitude that changes little over
one bounce period.

$\omega_b$ is compared with two rates, and each comparison is a different nonlinear scale.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mu=\omega_b/\Delta\Omega$: trapping versus phase mixing of the \emph{continuum}. If $\mu\ll1$
the particles dephase before they feel the well; if $\mu\gtrsim1$ islands form across the
whole band.
\item $\nu=\omega_b/\gamma$: trapping versus damping of the \emph{mode}. It is O'Neil's parameter
(1965, Phys.\ Fluids 8, 2255) for Landau damping in plasmas. If $\nu\ll1$ the wave is damped
before it traps, and linear theory holds at all times. If $\nu\gtrsim1$, from
$t\sim2\pi/\omega_b$ on, the resonant particles are trapped, damping stops, and the amplitude
oscillates at a frequency $\sim\omega_b$ and saturates. The final state, with a plateau of $F$
around $J_r$, is not predicted by linear theory, which holds only for $t\lesssim1/\omega_b$.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Approximation 9: the amplitude of $\delta\Phi$.} The naive estimate
$|\delta\Phi|\sim\varepsilon\,|\Phi_{\rm self}|$ is wrong by a large factor. A $\cos Q$ perturbation bunches
the particles in phase: it does not change the mass of the shell, it only
\emph{displaces} it radially by a fraction $\sim\varepsilon R_e$ of its radius, and the potential
of a displaced shell differs from the original one by $\sim\varepsilon\,(R_e/r_c)\,|\Phi_{\rm self}|$.
Section~\ref{sec:res_escalas} confirms this with a reference run. In practice no estimate
is needed: the linear solution of each case gives $\delta\Phi(r,t)$ with $\varepsilon=1$, and
\[
  \omega_b \approx \sqrt{|\Omega'|\,\max_r|\delta\Phi(r,0)|},\qquad |\Omega'|\approx\Delta\Omega/J_t ,
\]
with $k=1$ and the mean slope of $\Omega(J)$ instead of its value at the resonance. For
another $\varepsilon$, $\omega_b$ scales as $\sqrt\varepsilon$.

\paragraph{The transition is nonlinear.} \emph{Approximation 10: O'Neil's criterion.} It is
a result from plasma physics (one wave, one dimension), and here it is used as a scale,
not as a quantitative prediction: it is what the PIC runs test.

As $\eta\to\eta_c$ the damping rate $\gamma\to0$, while $\omega_b$, which is set by the amplitude, stays
finite. So $\nu\to\infty$ for any finite amplitude: \textbf{linear theory predicts where the
transition is, but near it does not describe the late-time evolution for any finite
amplitude.} This is precisely the regime to be studied, and it is where the code is
indispensable. On the other side ($\eta>\eta_c$) the mode is discrete and resonates with no
particle, so O'Neil's mechanism does not act, and the nonlinear question is a different
one: whether the mode survives at finite amplitude. Section~\ref{sec:res_escalas} gives the
values of $\nu$ for the cases considered.

\paragraph{State of the theory.} The results of Hadžić, Rein, Schrecker and Straub
concern the \emph{linearized} problem at small mass ($\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0$): they state when
$\delta\Phi$ decays and when there are pure oscillations, for the linear dynamics around the
equilibrium. The closest nonlinear numerical work they cite is that of Ramming and
Rein (2018), for the radial problem without a central mass. To our knowledge, whether
this damping persists in the nonlinear gravitational problem is an open question. In
plasmas there is the theorem of Mouhot and Villani (2011), for small and very regular
perturbations; in gravity we know of no equivalent, and O'Neil's argument shows why the
question is delicate precisely near the transition. This is the question that the code
can address and linear theory cannot.

\paragraph{Consequences for the design.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The linear questions are answered with \texttt{lineal.py}: the linear calculations of
Section~\ref{sec:lineal} are done, not proposed.
\item The PIC runs concentrate where $\nu\gtrsim1$, with the linear solution of the same
equilibrium as reference at each point. What is measured is the \emph{deviation} from it,
as a function of $\nu$ and of time.
\item The perturbative form of the initial condition becomes an advantage. As a sentence
for the paper: \emph{we use $F=F_{\rm eq}(1+\varepsilon s\chi)$ not because we assume linearity,
but because it is a controlled family in which the linear prediction is computed
exactly, so that the nonlinear deviation is measured without ambiguity.}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Numerical methods}
\label{sec:metodos}

\subsection{Self-consistent equilibria}
\label{sec:metodo_equilibrio}

\texttt{equilibrio.py} constructs the equilibrium by iterating
\[
  \Phi_{\rm self}\;\to\;\text{map }(r,p_r)\to(Q,J)\;\to\;F_{\rm eq}(J)\;\to\;\rho(r)\;
  \to\;\text{Poisson}\;\to\;\Phi_{\rm self},
\]
starting from $\Phi_{\rm self}=0$, until the potential changes by less than $10^{-13}$ between
iterations. The angle--action map of each iterate is computed numerically
(\texttt{aa\_\allowbreak numerico.py}): for each energy the turning points $r_\pm$ are found by
bisection, and the integrals are computed with Gauss--Legendre quadrature after the
substitution $r = r_m + r_a\sin\theta$, where $r_m$ and $r_a$ are the centre and half-width of
$[r_-,r_+]$. At a turning point $E-\Phi_{\rm ef}$ vanishes linearly in $r$, so
$p_r\propto|r-r_\pm|^{1/2}$: the integrand of $J$ has an infinite derivative there, and that of
the radial period, $1/p_r$, is infinite. Gauss--Legendre quadrature converges slowly for both.
With the substitution, $E-\Phi_{\rm ef}=r_a^2\cos^2\theta\,h(\theta)$ with $h$ smooth and positive,
so $p_r\,dr/d\theta=r_a^2\cos^2\theta\sqrt{2h}$ and $(dr/d\theta)/p_r=1/\sqrt{2h}$ are smooth, and
64 nodes give $J$ to $10^{-14}$ in the bare isochrone (Section~2.1 of the demo document).
$E(J)$ is tabulated, and $\Omega = dE/dJ$ is obtained by finite differences.

\paragraph{Convergence.} This is a fixed-point (Picard) iteration without relaxation. If
the map is a contraction near the solution, with factor $q<1$, the iteration converges
geometrically, the fixed point is unique in that neighbourhood, and the error after the
last iteration is at most $q/(1-q)$ times the last change. For small mass the contraction
holds, with $q=O(a_0)$: $\Phi_{\rm self}$ is the potential of a fixed mass, and a change of the
potential only redistributes that mass. For finite mass, \texttt{equilibrio.py} measures $q$
as the median ratio of consecutive changes and reports the bound. For the Wilson and King
equilibria used in the PIC runs, $q$ grows from $0.017$ at $a_0=0.0065$ to $0.587$ at $a_0=0.5$,
following $q\approx a_0/(a_0+0.35)$ for $g=2$, and the bound is below $10^{-13}$. The script stops
with an error if the change is still above the tolerance after 60 iterations, which by the
fit would happen near $a_0\approx0.7$.

\paragraph{An independent construction.} For the lowered Maxwellian, which depends on the
energy only, the density at $r$ depends only on the potential at $r$:
$4\pi r^2\rho=8\pi^2L_0A\sqrt{2\pi T}\,E_\gamma\big(g+\tfrac12,(E_t-\Phi_{\rm ef})/T\big)$. Poisson's equation is then
an ordinary differential equation, integrated over the support, and three numbers
($E_t$, $T$ and $A$) are fixed by the mass, $W_0$ and $J_t$ (\texttt{equilibrio\_\allowbreak edo.py};
\texttt{equilibrio.py -{}-metodo edo}). The two constructions agree to $1$--$2\times10^{-6}$ in
$\Phi_{\rm self}$. The difference is the discretization error of the iteration, which
decreases as the square of its radial spacing. Evolved with the PIC code at $\varepsilon=0$, the
equilibria of the two constructions agree to better than \SI{1}{\%}. Their departures from
stationarity come from the PIC discretization. There are two effects: an $O(\Delta r^2)$ error
of the field, and a slow spread of the actions caused by the discreteness of the particles,
which disappears with four times more particles. The derivation and the tables are in Section~2.1 of the demo
document (\texttt{docs/\allowbreak demo\_\allowbreak eta/\allowbreak demo\_\allowbreak eta.pdf}), and
the PIC tests in its Section~5.2.

The initial data use nodes in $[0,J_{\max}]$ (Section~\ref{sec:discreto}). With compact
support $J_{\max}=J_t$, the edge of the support; for the Gaussian, $J_{\max}=6\sigma_J$.

\subsection{Particle loading}
\label{sec:discreto}

The particles are placed on a regular grid of the true angle--action variables of the
equilibrium:
\[
  J_i = \big(i-\tfrac12\big)\frac{J_{\max}}{N_{rc}},\quad i=1\ldots N_{rc};\qquad
  Q_j = \big(j-\tfrac12\big)\frac{2\pi}{N_{pc}},\quad j=1\ldots N_{pc}.
\]
Each node is a particle of weight $8\pi^2L_0\,F(Q_j,J_i)\,\Delta Q\,\Delta J$, and its position
$(r,p_r)$ is obtained by inverting the map of the equilibrium. There are $N=N_{rc}N_{pc}$
particles. This is a quiet start: there is no random sampling noise, and the remaining
noise comes from the discretization. The same grid in $J$ sets the recurrence ceiling
(Section~\ref{sec:recurrencia}).

\subsection{Linearized solver}
\label{sec:metodo_lineal}

The linear calculations have two steps. \texttt{lineal.py} computes the time evolution of
the problem linearized about each equilibrium, without particles, and
\texttt{barrido\_\allowbreak lineal.py} extracts from the signal the collective mode: its
frequency, its damping rate and its position in the band (Section~\ref{sec:polo}).

\paragraph{The equations.} The problem is the linearized Vlasov--Poisson system of
Section~\ref{sec:respuesta}, written in the angle--action variables of the equilibrium:
\begin{align}
  &\partial_t\,\delta F + \Omega(J)\,\partial_Q\,\delta F
     = F_{\rm eq}'(J)\,\partial_Q\,\delta\Phi(Q,J,t), \label{eq:vlasov_lin}\\[2pt]
  &\delta\Phi(Q,J,t) = \delta\Phi\big(r(Q,J),t\big),\qquad
   \delta\Phi(r,t) = 8\pi^2L_0\iint dQ'\,dJ'\;G\big(r,r(Q',J')\big)\,\delta F(Q',J',t).
   \label{eq:poisson_lin}
\end{align}
The first equation is the evolution of the perturbation: free transport on the left, and
the push of the self-consistent field on the equilibrium on the right. The second is
Poisson's equation: the potential of the perturbation is the sum of the contributions of
all points of phase space, with the Green's function of shells $G(r,r')=-1/\max(r,r')$
(Section~\ref{sec:observables}). $r(Q,J)$ is the radius of the point $(Q,J)$ in the
equilibrium. In linear theory this map is fixed: the changes of the orbits produced by
$\delta\Phi$ are of second order, and they enter only through the term on the right.

\paragraph{Initial data.} $\delta F(Q,J,0)=F_{\rm eq}(J)\,s(J)\cos Q$, the perturbation of
Section~\ref{sec:perturbacion} with $\varepsilon=1$. Since the problem is linear, the response to
another $\varepsilon$ is $\varepsilon$ times this one, and all reported quantities ($h_1$, $\delta\Phi$, and
$\omega_b$ with $\varepsilon=1$) scale accordingly.

\paragraph{Time evolution rather than eigenvalues.} A Landau-damped mode \emph{is not} an
eigenvector of the linear operator: the spectrum on the real axis is the continuous band
plus, if they exist, the discrete modes below it. The damped mode is a pole of the
analytic continuation of the response, and computing it as an eigenvalue (the matrix
method of Kalnajs) requires that continuation. In the time evolution it appears by
itself, as a term $e^{-\gamma t}$ in the late signal, together with the algebraic tails of the
continuum (Section~\ref{sec:regularidad}); a discrete mode appears as an oscillation of
constant amplitude. Solving the evolution and fitting the tail gives both without
analytic continuation, and it is directly comparable with the PIC run.

\paragraph{Discretization.} The solver uses the same regular grid as the PIC initial data
(Section~\ref{sec:discreto}), refined:
\[
  J_i=\big(i-\tfrac12\big)\frac{J_{\max}}{N_J},\quad
  Q_j=\big(j-\tfrac12\big)\frac{2\pi}{N_Q},\qquad N_J\times N_Q=1600\times32 .
\]
The unknown is $\delta F_{ij}(t)=\delta F(Q_j,J_i,t)$. What the method needs from the equilibrium is
computed once:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{the radii} $r_{ij}=r(Q_j,J_i)$, by inverting the angle--action map of the
equilibrium (\texttt{equilibrio.\allowbreak invertir}): $51\,200$ inversions, about \SI{16}{s},
stored in a file;
\item \textbf{the frequencies} $\Omega_i=\Omega(J_i)=dE/dJ$, from the table $E(J)$ of the equilibrium
by centred differences, which reproduce $\Omega=(J+c)^{-3}$ of the isochrone to $10^{-4}$;
\item \textbf{the derivative} $F_{\rm eq}'(J_i)$, analytic: that of the formula for the Gaussian
and the hollow family, and $F_{\rm eq}'=(dF_{\rm eq}/dE)\,\Omega(J)$ for the Maxwellian, which is
written in the energy.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Transport.} Without the term on the right, the solution is
$\delta F(Q,J,t+\Delta t)=\delta F(Q-\Omega(J)\Delta t,J,t)$: each row of fixed $J$ rotates in $Q$ without
deforming. In Fourier space this is a phase: with
$\widehat{\delta F}_{ik}=\frac{1}{N_Q}\sum_j\delta F_{ij}\,e^{-ikQ_j}$, the fast Fourier transform along
$Q$ at each $J_i$,
\[
  \widehat{\delta F}_{ik}(t+\Delta t) = e^{-ik\Omega_i\Delta t}\;\widehat{\delta F}_{ik}(t).
\]
It is exact for any $\Delta t$: there is no stability condition and no numerical
dispersion. The only errors come from the finite number of harmonics, $|k|<N_Q/2$, and the
finite number of rows in $J$, which sets the recurrence ceiling
$T_{\rm rec}(k)=N_J\tau_1/k$ (Section~\ref{sec:recurrencia}): with $N_J=1600$ it is $1600\,\tau_1$ for
$k=1$, far beyond the runs.

\paragraph{Field kick.} Without the transport, the equation is
$\partial_t\delta F=F_{\rm eq}'(J)\,\partial_Q\delta\Phi$. Over a short interval $h$, with $\delta\Phi$ fixed,
\[
  \delta F_{ij} \;\leftarrow\; \delta F_{ij} + h\,F_{\rm eq}'(J_i)\,\partial_Q\delta\Phi(Q_j,J_i),
\]
with the derivative in $Q$ also computed in Fourier space: transform along $Q$, multiply
by $ik$ and transform back.

\paragraph{Poisson's equation at the nodes.} Each node represents a spherical shell of
mass $m_{ij}=8\pi^2L_0\,\delta F_{ij}\,\Delta Q\,\Delta J$ at radius $r_{ij}$: the set of particles at that
radius with all possible orientations of the angular momentum. The potential of a set of
shells is exact with the Green's function of shells: at the radius of node $l$,
\[
  \delta\Phi(r_l) = -\frac{1}{r_l}\sum_{r_n<r_l}m_n \;-\; \sum_{r_n>r_l}\frac{m_n}{r_n}
  \;-\;\frac{m_l}{r_l},
\]
where the first term is the enclosed mass, the second the exterior shells and the third
the node's own shell. $G$ is continuous at $r'=r$ (it is $-1/r$ on both sides; its
derivative has the kink), so the contribution of the own shell is unambiguous. The code
splits it half into each sum, which gives the same result and treats nodes with equal
radii symmetrically. The masses can be negative, because $\delta F$ is a perturbation. Since
the radii do not change, the ordering of the nodes by radius is computed once, and each
evaluation of $\delta\Phi$ consists of two cumulative sums over the $N_JN_Q$ nodes. This $\delta\Phi$
at each node is the one used in the kick; for the output it is also interpolated at 121
radii in $r\in[3,15]$.

\paragraph{The full step.} The two operations are combined by Strang splitting: half a
kick, a full transport step and another half kick,
\[
  \delta F(t+\Delta t) = \mathcal K_{\Delta t/2}\,\mathcal T_{\Delta t}\,\mathcal K_{\Delta t/2}\;\delta F(t),
\]
which is second order in $\Delta t$. The error per step comes from the fact that transport
and kick do not commute, and it is of the order of the square of the product of $\Delta t$ and
the scale of the coupling. With $\Delta t=0.5$ it is negligible, as checked by halving
$\Delta t$. With the option \texttt{-{}-libre} the kicks are omitted, which gives the exact free
phase mixing of Section~\ref{sec:mezcla}. A case of $10\,\tau_1$ takes between about 2\,000
and 22\,000 steps, depending on $\tau_1$, and costs one to three minutes.

\paragraph{Recorded quantities.} Every two time units the solver stores:
\begin{itemize}
\item $h_1(t)=\sum_{ij}\delta F_{ij}\,B(J_i)\,e^{-iQ_j}\big/\big(N_Q\sum_iF_{\rm eq}(J_i)\,B(J_i)\big)$, with the
test function of Section~\ref{sec:observables}, and $h_2$ with $e^{-2iQ_j}$. With the flat
perturbation $h_1(0)=\tfrac12$. This is exactly the analogue of $[h_1(\varepsilon)-h_1(0)]/\varepsilon$ in
the PIC runs: the same frame, the same $B$ and the same normalization, which is why the
two agree to four digits in 11\_landau;
\item $\delta\Phi(r,t)$ at the 121 radii, with the amplitude of $\varepsilon=1$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Extraction of the mode}
\label{sec:polo}

\emph{Approximation 11: the dominant pole.} The mode is extracted from the late signal
with the \emph{matrix pencil} method, the same fit used for 11\_landau. We take $N$ samples
of $h_1$ in a window, $y_n=h_1(t_0+n\Delta)$, and represent them as a sum of $M$ exponentials,
\[
  y_n \approx \sum_{m=1}^{M} a_m\,z_m^n,\qquad z_m=e^{s_m\Delta}.
\]
The samples form the Hankel matrix $Y_{il}=y_{i+l}$, with $i=0\ldots N-L-1$ and $l=0\ldots L$,
$L=N/2$. If the model were exact, each row would be a combination of the $M$ vectors
$(1,z_m,z_m^2,\ldots,z_m^L)$, and $Y$ would have rank $M$. The singular value decomposition
$Y=U\Sigma V^H$ gives that subspace: the first $M$ columns of $V$. Calling $V_1$ these columns
without their last row and $V_2$ without their first row, a shift by one place amounts to
a multiplication by the $z_m$, and the $z_m$ are the eigenvalues of $V_1^+V_2$ ($V_1^+$ is the
pseudoinverse). Then $s_m=\ln z_m/\Delta$, and the amplitudes $a_m$ follow by least squares.
The mode is the term of largest amplitude, with
\[
  \omega=-\mathrm{Im}\,s,\qquad \gamma=-\mathrm{Re}\,s ,
\]
because the signal behaves as $e^{-i\omega t-\gamma t}$. We use $M=3$: one term for the mode and two
to absorb what remains of the continuum.

\paragraph{Windows.} The fit is repeated in windows $[a,2a]\,\tau_1$ with $a=1$, $2$, $3$, $5$, $10$,
$20$ and $30$, those covered by the run, subsampled to at most 1000 points (the Hankel
matrix grows as $N^2$; with $\omega\approx0.08$ the resulting sampling remains well below the Nyquist
limit). Before $t=\tau_1$ the transient of phase mixing dominates, and it is not a pole. Two
consecutive windows \emph{agree} if their discrepancy,
\[
  \max\!\left(\frac{|\omega_1-\omega_2|}{\Delta\Omega},\;
  \frac{|\gamma_1-\gamma_2|}{\max(\bar\gamma,\,2\times10^{-3}\Delta\Omega)}\right),
\]
is below $0.2$; the floor $2\times10^{-3}\Delta\Omega$ avoids dividing by $\gamma\approx0$ for discrete modes. We
report the latest pair that agrees, because it is the asymptotic behaviour, and the value
is the mean of the pair. If no pair agrees, the tail is not a single exponential
(algebraic phase mixing, or two comparable components), and the case is marked ``no
dominant pole''. With $\omega$ and $\gamma$ we give $x=(\omega-\Omega_{\min})/\Delta\Omega$ and $\gamma/\Delta\Omega$, with the band
of the equilibrium.

\paragraph{Bounce frequency.} The linear $\delta\Phi(r,t)$ gives
$\omega_b=\sqrt{|\Omega'|\,\max_r|\delta\Phi|}$ with $|\Omega'|\approx\Delta\Omega/J_t$ (Approximation~9,
Section~\ref{sec:atrapamiento}), at $t=0$ and at the start of the fitting window. With $\gamma$
it gives $\nu=\omega_b/\gamma$ for any $\varepsilon$, since $\omega_b\propto\sqrt\varepsilon$.

\paragraph{Fit of $\delta\Phi$ as a control.} The same procedure is applied to $\delta\Phi(r,t)$,
which uses no test function. In each window we take the dominant time series of the
singular value decomposition of the matrix $\delta\Phi(r_n,t_m)$ and apply matrix pencil with
$M=4$; since the signal is real, the poles come in conjugate pairs, and we take the one
with $\omega>0$ of largest amplitude.

\subsection{Free-decay quadrature}
\label{sec:metodo_colas}

\emph{Approximation 12: tails in the bare isochrone.} The free decay of
Section~\ref{sec:res_colas} is computed with \texttt{colas\_\allowbreak libres.py}, which evaluates
the integrals of Section~\ref{sec:libre} by quadrature, without time evolution, in the
isochrone without self-gravity ($a_0\to0$): the tail is a property of the shape of $F_{\rm eq}$.
The quadrature reproduces to two digits the decay of \texttt{landau\_\allowbreak libre} (the sum
over the particles of 11\_landau) over $t=400$--$1400$, and changes by less than
$3\times10^{-4}$ with half the grid in $J$ up to $40\,\tau_1$.

\subsection{Reference runs}
\label{sec:landau_def}

The runs of Section~11 of the notes (11\_landau) serve as a validated reference: a
Gaussian with $\sigma_J=0.10$ and $J_{\max}=0.6$, $a_0=0.01$, $L_0=2$, a perturbation $\varepsilon=0.1$
with $\chi=\cos Q$, $N_{rc}\times N_{pc}=400\times25$ ($N=10^4$), a grid $r\in[0,20]$ with $\Delta r=0.1$,
$\Delta t=0.1$ (\texttt{courant}\,$=2$), the yoshida4 integrator and the test function
$j_1=s_{j1}=0.10$. Their $\eta$ is $0.05$, deep in the damped regime. The run
\texttt{landau\_\allowbreak libre} evolves the same perturbation without self-gravity, in the
potential of the equilibrium.

\item A distribution with $F_{\rm eq}(0)>0$ and perturbation $s=1$ leaves in $\delta\Phi$ a tail 150

\item \textbf{The local-density term dominates}: with $\sigma_J=0.05$ it contributes

\subsection{The Landau runs}
\label{sec:landau}

11\_landau has $\eta\approx0.05$, deep in the damped regime. Its results illustrate the
framework.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The tail is damped, as expected for $\eta\ll1$.} The fit of the tail gives
$\omega = 0.0570$ and $\gamma = 5.1\times10^{-3}$, stable over three time windows, three orders of
the fit, and between $N_{rc}=400$ and $800$.
\item \textbf{The frequency is inside the band, near its lower edge.} With $a_0=10^{-2}$
the band is $[0.0522,\,0.0714]$ and $x=0.25$. This is what the Antonov form predicts: the
attractive coupling pushes the pole towards $\Omega_{\min}$ but is not strong enough to pull it
out.
\item \textbf{Self-gravity changes the \emph{form} of the decay.} Without it the decay of
$h_1$ is Gaussian (Table~\ref{tab:libre}); with it the tail is exponential with rate $\gamma$,
and at late times the exponential dominates the Gaussian: the ratio between the signals
with and without self-gravity goes from $1$ to $\sim30$ by $t=1600$.
\item \textbf{The regime is linear.} The PIC run agrees with linear theory to four
digits ($0.41998$ against $0.41994$ at $t=200$).
\item \textbf{The numerical floor limits the tail.} The run with $\varepsilon=0$ gives the noise
level, $\approx10^{-4}$ in the same units. The tail can be followed from $t\approx1000$ to
$t\approx1600$, about three $e$-foldings of $\gamma$.
\end{itemize}
The framework then predicts that, as $\eta$ increases, the mode moves down towards
$\Omega_{\min}$ ($x\to0$) and $\gamma$ decreases; once the mode lies below the band ($x<0$), $\gamma$ should
be zero within the error. Section~\ref{sec:barrido} confirms it.

\subsection{Checks of the linear solver}
\label{sec:comprobaciones}

\paragraph{Validation.} The case of 11\_landau reproduces \emph{bit for bit} the stored
linear solution and gives $\omega=0.0570$, $\gamma=5.0\times10^{-3}$ and $x=0.25$, as does the fit of
the PIC run. The linear solution also gives $|\delta\Phi|_{\max}=2.3\times10^{-4}$ with $\varepsilon=1$, that is,
$2.3\times10^{-5}$ with $\varepsilon=0.1$, which is exactly what the PIC run measured.

\paragraph{Convergence.} In A4 ($\eta=1$) the pole does not change when $\Delta t$ is halved,
with $N_J=800$, or with $N_Q$ doubled: $\gamma=3.66\times10^{-4}$ against $3.64\times10^{-4}$, and $\omega$ agrees
to five digits. The discrete modes give $|\gamma|\lesssim10^{-5}$ with either sign, also with
$\Delta t$ halved: this is the floor of the fit, not a growth (the main family cannot grow,
by Antonov's theorem).

\paragraph{The pole does not depend on the test function.} $B(J)$ is a choice of the
code (Section~\ref{sec:observables}); a weight $B(J)\,e^{-ikQ}$ only needs to be continuous at
the circular orbit, which requires $B$ to vanish at least as $J^{k/2}$, and $J^2$ satisfies
this up to $k=4$. Repeating A4 and L5 with $B\propto\sqrt J\,e^{-(J-0.6J_t)^2/(0.6J_t)^2}$ gives the
same $\omega$ to four digits and $\gamma$ to \SI{9}{\%} in A4 ($3.34$ against $3.66\times10^{-4}$); the fit of
$\delta\Phi$, which does not use $B$, gives $3.14\times10^{-4}$. $\gamma$ therefore carries an uncertainty
of $\sim$\SI{10}{\%}. The fit of $\delta\Phi$ works only where the pole dominates: at a fixed
radius, $\delta\Phi$ carries the algebraic tails of the turning points and of the edge
(Section~\ref{sec:colasdphi}), which a sum of exponentials cannot represent, and $h_1$ does
not have them precisely because $B$ is smooth and vanishes at $J=0$.

criterion ``$\omega$ outside the band'' is well defined only with compact support.

This is the direction of the dichotomy of Section~\ref{sec:borde}: a sharp edge gives
oscillations, a smooth edge gives damping. An interpretation, which the calculation
supports but does not prove: the dispersion relation contains
$\int F_{\rm eq}'(J)\,(\ldots)/(\Omega(J)-\omega)\,dJ$, and near the edge $F_{\rm eq}'\propto(J_t-J)^{g-1}$. For
$g\le1$ the integral diverges as $\omega\to\Omega_{\min}$, so an arbitrarily weak coupling would suffice
to pull a mode below the edge, like a bound state at the edge of a band. For $g>1$ it
converges, and a finite coupling is needed: a threshold $\eta_c(g)$ that grows with $g$. In
this sense $\eta_c$ is a numerical estimate of the $\varepsilon_0(2)$ of the theorem for the
isochrone background, expressed in the variable $\eta$.

\subsection{Questions left for the PIC runs}
\label{sec:dejaPIC}

Linear theory answers the linear questions: where the transition is and how it depends
on $\eta$, on the width, on the shape and on the edge. It leaves open two nonlinear
questions, precisely near the transition.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{On the damped side} ($\eta\lesssim\eta_c$), $\gamma\to0$ and $\nu=\omega_b/\gamma$ grows without
bound for any amplitude, so linear theory fails at late times
(Section~\ref{sec:objecion}). The question is which final state the system selects:
damping, saturation or sustained oscillation.
\item \textbf{On the discrete side} ($\eta>\eta_c$) there are no resonant particles and O'Neil's
mechanism does not act. The question is whether the mode survives at finite amplitude:
its frequency shifts with the amplitude, and a mode just below the edge (L5, $x=-0.01$)
can enter the band and be damped, which a separated mode (A5, $x=-0.07$) should not
suffer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Design of the PIC battery}
\label{sec:diseno}

\subsection{Choice of the distribution}
\label{sec:familia}

The main family is the Wilson lowered Maxwellian (Section~\ref{sec:equilibrio}) with $g=2$
and $W_0=3$, with the perturbation \texttt{suave3}. The alternatives are the Gaussian of
the Landau runs and the hollow family.

\paragraph{Why not the Gaussian.} The Gaussian $J^2e^{-J^2/\sigma_J^2}$ was proposed for its
resemblance to a Maxwell--Boltzmann distribution, but it is not a Maxwellian. In the
Maxwell distribution of speeds, $4\pi v^2e^{-v^2/2\sigma^2}$, the factor $v^2$ is the Jacobian of
the velocity shell; the distribution function itself is $e^{-v^2/2\sigma^2}$, maximal at
$v=0$. Here the measure is already flat, $dr\,dp_r=dQ\,dJ$, so the $J^2$ of $F$ is not a
Jacobian but a genuine depletion of phase space around the circular orbit. At $r=r_c$,
where $J\approx p_r^2/2\kappa$ (Section~\ref{sec:aa}), it gives a distribution of $p_r$
proportional to $p_r^4e^{-p_r^4/4\kappa^2\sigma_J^2}$: zero at $p_r=0$ and with two maxima, two
crossing radial streams. It also has a population inversion, $\partial F/\partial E>0$ for
$J<\sigma_J$, so Antonov's theorem does not guarantee its stability.

\paragraph{The correct analogue.} $F\propto e^{-E/T}$. Since $E=p_r^2/2+\Phi_{\rm ef}(r)$, at each
radius it gives \emph{exactly} a Gaussian in $p_r$ with dispersion $\sqrt T$ and a Boltzmann
density $e^{-\Phi_{\rm ef}/T}$, and since $E=E(J)$ it is an exact equilibrium. Near the circular
orbit $E\approx E_c+\kappa J$, and $F\propto e^{-\kappa J/\sigma_R^2}$, the quasi-isothermal distribution of
Binney (2010, MNRAS 401, 2318), standard for warm discs in action variables. The width
has a physical meaning, $J_0=\sigma_R^2/\kappa$, with $\sigma_R=\sqrt T$ the radial velocity dispersion;
with $W_0=3$ and $J_t=0.138$, $\sigma_R/v_c=0.16$ relative to the circular velocity
$v_c=L_0/r_c$: a warm shell.

\paragraph{Compact support from tides.} A cluster in an external field loses the stars
with $E>E_t$. The family of lowered isothermal models of Gieles and Zocchi (2015, MNRAS
454, 576), which is $E_\gamma(g,x)$, describes this cut: King (1966) with $g=1$ and Wilson (1975)
with $g=2$. Its exponent $g$ is the one of the Hadžić dichotomy, and the family contains
as limits the polytrope, $(E_t-E)^g$, when $W_0\to0$, and the isothermal sphere when
$W_0\to\infty$.

The reasons for the choice, in order of importance, are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{It is monotonic}, $\partial F/\partial E<0$: by Antonov's theorem it has no growing
radial modes (Section~\ref{sec:antonov}), and an oscillation that does not decay is a
genuine neutral mode, without the ambiguity of an instability. It also satisfies the
monotonicity hypothesis under which the theory of Hadžić and collaborators is
formulated; the two hollow families do not.
\item \textbf{Its interior is thermal}: at each radius the distribution of $p_r$ is Gaussian,
lowered near $E_t$. With $W_0=3$ the maximum is \SI{80}{\%} of that of the untruncated
isothermal model, $P(2,3)=0.80$. Its parameters have a physical meaning: temperature,
tidal energy and the shape of the edge.
\item \textbf{Its compact support has a physical cause}, the tides, and it leaves the band
exact: the essential spectrum and $\eta$ are properties of the equilibrium, not of a
threshold.
\item \textbf{Its edge is the one of the Hadžić dichotomy, and it is adjustable}: $g=2$ is in
the damped class, and the edge experiment is the same distribution with another $g$,
including King ($g=1$), the marginal case.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Why $g=2$ and $W_0=3$.} We explored $g\in\{2,3\}$ and $W_0\in\{1,\ldots,8\}$ with
$J_t=0.138$ (Table~\ref{tab:barrido}). All give $\eta=1$ with $a_0$ between $0.066$ and $0.074$;
what changes is the shape. Raising $W_0$ makes the core more thermal, but it leaves the
edge without weight and weakens the smooth perturbation; at the same $W_0$, $g=2$ has a more
thermal core, a lower tail and more potential than $g=3$. $W_0=3$ is the compromise: a core
at \SI{80}{\%} of the isothermal one, and \SI{7}{\%} of the mass in the outer half of the
support, which is where the edge lives. $W_0=6$, the nearly thermal limit, enters as case
E3 and run V2.

\paragraph{The cost: the edge at $\Omega_{\max}$.} Every monotonic distribution is maximal at
$J=0$, so the upper edge of the band, $\Omega_{\max}=\kappa(r_c)$, carries weight. This has three
consequences.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] The flat perturbation is discontinuous at the circular orbit
(Section~\ref{sec:perturbacion}), and it leaves in $\delta\Phi$ a tail $t^{-3/2}$ at $\Omega_{\max}$, 150
times above the Gaussian at $10\,\tau_1$. With $s=(J/J_t)^{3/2}$ the tail drops to the level
of the Gaussian (Table~\ref{tab:colas}).
\item[(b)] That smooth perturbation produces little potential, $\rho_\Phi=0.11$: for the same
$\delta\Phi$ it needs an $\varepsilon$ nine times larger than the Gaussian. This does not limit the
nonlinear study, because near the transition the controlling parameter is
$\nu=\omega_b/\gamma$, which is reached with $\varepsilon\ll1$ (Section~\ref{sec:objecion}).
\item[(c)] Self-gravity regenerates a component at $\Omega_{\max}$ with tail $t^{-2}$
(Section~\ref{sec:regenerada}) that no initial condition removes. It is fitted as a
separate component, with known frequency and exponent, and its amplitude is computed
beforehand with \texttt{lineal.py}.
\end{enumerate}
In addition, the band widens with the mass (Section~\ref{sec:res_corrimiento}), so the same
$\eta$ describes a different geometry than in the hollow families. Case E1 measures whether
this matters, and it does (Section~\ref{sec:barrido}).

\paragraph{Why not the pure polytrope.} $(E_t-E)^g$ is the limit $W_0\to0$ of this family: it
keeps the three costs above and loses the thermal interior.

The cost per run is almost independent of $L_0$: the number of steps needed to cover the

$L_0=2$, $1$ and $0.25$. We take $L_0=2$ as the reference, for continuity with the runs,
equilibria and analyses already validated, and $L_0=1$ as a control, with the linear cases
D1 and D2 and the PIC run V1. If the result collapses in $\eta$ for two values of $L_0$, the
claim no longer depends on that choice: $L_0$ changes at once the enclosed background mass,
the radius, the period and the shape of $\Omega(J)$.

A fair comparison requires scaling the width, because what matters is
$\Delta\Omega/\Omega\approx 3\,\Delta J/c$: \textbf{the width of the support must scale as $c$}. The
equivalent of $J_t=0.138$ with $L_0=2$ is $J_t=0.094$ with $L_0=1$ ($\sigma_J=0.034$). The grid must
also scale with $r_c$: with $L_0=1$ the system lives at $r\sim1.5$--$4$, so $\Delta r=0.05$ and
$r_{\max}=8$ give the same relative resolution as $\Delta r=0.1$ and $r_{\max}=20$ with $L_0=2$.

\paragraph{Why vary the width and not only the mass.}
\label{sec:ancho}
With the width of the Landau runs ($J_t=0.276$, or $\sigma_J=0.10$) the shift is linear in the
mass, $\delta\Omega/\Omega\approx 2.1\,a_0$ for the Maxwellian, while the width of the band is $\approx 0.31$.
The masses $10^{-3}$, $10^{-2}$ and $10^{-1}$ then give $\eta\approx0.007$, $0.07$ and $0.68$, \emph{all
three on the same side}. Such a scan would measure damping in all three cases and would
not distinguish between ``self-gravity is not enough'' and ``the regime was not
reached''. With $J_t=0.083$ the band without mass shrinks to $0.10$, and the mass $a_0=0.044$
already gives $\eta\approx 1.0$: \textbf{the width of the support is the parameter that allows
the transition to be crossed at small masses}.

One could instead raise $a_0$ up to $0.3$ or $0.5$ at $J_t=0.276$, and the transition would also
be crossed. We do not, for three reasons: (i) it mixes two changes, since raising the mass
shifts the frequencies \emph{and} deforms the equilibrium, so the transition cannot be
attributed to a single cause; (ii) it leaves the stated regime, because with $a_0=0.5$ the
self-gravitating component is comparable to the enclosed background mass; and (iii) it
increases the risk of instability (Section~\ref{sec:riesgos}).

\paragraph{Duration of the runs and the recurrence ceiling.}
\label{sec:recurrencia}
Two scales set the duration. The first is phase mixing: the linear runs last
$t_{\rm fin}=10\,\tau_1$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_libre}), and the nonlinear runs also several
bounce periods (Section~\ref{sec:bateria}).

The second is \emph{recurrence}, an artefact of the regular grid in $J$. Two neighbouring
nodes, separated by $\Delta J=J_{\max}/N_{rc}$, have frequencies that differ by
$|d\Omega/dJ|\,\Delta J$, and their harmonic $k$ accumulates a phase difference
$k\,|d\Omega/dJ|\,\Delta J\,t$. When this difference reaches $2\pi$, all pairs of neighbours are in
phase again: the grid cannot represent structure finer than $\Delta J$, and phase mixing
undoes itself. This happens at
\[
  T_{\rm rec}(k) \;=\; \frac{2\pi}{k\,|d\Omega/dJ|\,\Delta J} .
\]
Beyond $T_{\rm rec}$ any measured plateau is an artefact, and the limit is \emph{different for
each $k$}: it decreases as $1/k$. This is the effect of Birdsall and Langdon seen from the
other side.

With compact support and $J_{\max}=J_t$ the count simplifies: the mean slope is
$|d\Omega/dJ|\approx\Delta\Omega/J_t$ and $\Delta J=J_t/N_{rc}$, so
\[
  T_{\rm rec}(k) = \frac{N_{rc}}{k}\,\tau_1 ,
\]
independent of the width and of the mass. With $N_{rc}=400$, $T_{\rm rec}(1)=400\,\tau_1$, which in
A4 is $1.6\times10^5$, above the longest run ($5.2\times10^4$). For $k=4$ the ceiling drops to
$100\,\tau_1\approx4\times10^4$, and in N6 and in its reference with $\varepsilon=0$ one must state up to
when $h_4$ is valid. For the Gaussian (case E2), $J_{\max}=6\sigma_J$ covers 2.17 times the
support and the margin shrinks by that factor. This is why $J_{\max}$ must follow the
support and not stay at its historical value of $0.6$, which with $\sigma_J=0.03$ would waste
most nodes where $F\approx0$. The generator uses by default $J_{\max}=J_t$ for the compact
families and $6\sigma_J$ for the Gaussian.

\paragraph{Resolution.}
\label{sec:resolucion}
There are four independent sources of error, and they do not substitute for one
another:
\begin{itemize}
\item $N_{rc}$ (nodes in $J$) sets the time window before recurrence and how many nodes a
trapping island covers;
\item $N_{pc}$ (nodes in $Q$) sets how many modes $k$ are reliable; convergence in $Q$ is
spectral, and $25$ resolves well up to $k=4$;
\item $\Delta r$ sets the error of the self-gravity (deposition and Poisson solver);
\item $\Delta t$ sets the error of the time integrator.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Measurement protocol}
\label{sec:protocolo}

\paragraph{Linear reference.} Before each PIC run the linear problem is solved on the
same equilibrium (\texttt{lineal.py}, or \texttt{barrido\_\allowbreak lineal.py} for the cases of
this document). It gives the linear $h_k$ and $\delta\Phi$ for $\varepsilon=1$, the pole
$(\omega,\gamma)$, the regenerated component at $\Omega_{\max}$ and $\omega_b$. In 11\_landau the PIC run
agreed with it to four digits.

\paragraph{Observables of each PIC run.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The deviation from linear theory},
$D(t)=\|\delta\Phi_{\rm PIC}-\varepsilon\,\delta\Phi_{\rm lin}\|/\|\varepsilon\,\delta\Phi_{\rm lin}\|$, with $\delta\Phi$ the
perturbation of the potential with respect to the equilibrium
(\texttt{landau\_\allowbreak analisis.py}). \emph{It does not depend on any angle--action map.}
The time $t_{\rm nl}$ at which $D$ crosses $0.1$ is compared with $2\pi/\omega_b$: O'Neil predicts
$t_{\rm nl}\propto\varepsilon^{-1/2}$.
\item \textbf{The late envelope of $\delta\Phi$}: damping (linear), saturation (the ratio
$A_{\rm sat}/A_0$ between the final and initial amplitudes) and oscillation of the envelope,
whose frequency is compared with $\omega_b$. For the main family the fit also includes the
component at $\Omega_{\max}$, with its amplitude taken from the linear reference.
\item \textbf{The plateau of $F$ at the resonance}: the average over $Q$ of $F$ around $J_r$, with
$\Omega(J_r)=\omega$, should flatten over a width $\sim\Delta J_{\rm atr}$. It is the direct signature of
trapping, and it requires mapping the particles to the angle--action frame of the
equilibrium (\texttt{aa\_\allowbreak numerico.py}).
\item \textbf{$h_k(t)$ for $k=0\ldots4$} (Section~\ref{sec:observables}): a frequency common to
all $k$ is the signature of a mode; the generation of $k=2$ with amplitude $\propto\varepsilon^2$ is
the signature of the nonlinearity.
\item \textbf{The drift of $J$ and energy conservation}, which bound the time window in which
the result belongs to the system and not to the method.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Risks and controls}
\label{sec:riesgos}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Instability instead of oscillation.} The main family is monotonic and, by
Antonov's theorem, has no growing radial modes. The hollow family and the Gaussian do
increase between $J=0$ and their maximum ($\partial F/\partial E>0$ there): in them a growth is
reported as an instability, not as ``no damping''.
\item \textbf{Discretization floor.} The points with $\nu\approx1$ use a small $\varepsilon$ and a small
signal. The floor must lie clearly below the observed amplitude for several bounce
periods; this is the role of run B5, with $N=5\times10^4$ on the point with the smallest
signal.
\item \textbf{Resolution of the island.} The half-width $\Delta J_{\rm atr}=2\omega_b/|\Omega'|$ must cover
many nodes in $J$: Table~\ref{tab:mapa} gives the ratio $\Delta J_{\rm atr}/\Delta J$ for each point.
\item \textbf{Recurrence.} Any plateau measured beyond $T_{\rm rec}(k)=N_{rc}\tau_1/k$ is an artefact
of the grid in $J$. The long runs of block N stay below $T_{\rm rec}(1)$; for $k=4$ one must
state up to which $t$ it holds.
\item \textbf{Numerical drift of the equilibrium.} If $J$ drifts appreciably within the
window, what is measured is numerical relaxation and not trapping. This is the actual
limit of the useful window of the long runs, and it is controlled with $\varepsilon=0$ on the same
equilibrium.
\item \textbf{Nearly circular nodes.} In the main family the nodes with $J\to0$ have the
largest weights, and the map is degenerate there. The guards on the radicands (item E13
of the audit) cover the case, but each run must be checked for discarded particles.
\item \textbf{Edge tail in the main family.} The component at $\Omega_{\max}$ decays as $t^{-2}$; a
single-term fit would mistake it for a slow damping. It is fitted separately, with its
frequency fixed at the edge and its amplitude from the linear reference.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:nocubre}

The design does not cover the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Initial conditions far from an equilibrium}, such as the general function of
$Q$ of the draft or a cold collapse. There is no $F_{\rm eq}$ about which to linearize, and the
final state is not known in advance: it is the natural territory of the code, but it
requires its own design.
\item \textbf{The point-mass background} of the work of Hadžić and collaborators. The code can
reproduce it (\texttt{BGtype = sphere} is $-1/r$ outside unit radius), but the numerical
angle--action map is written around the isochrone, and the background would have to be
parametrized.
\item \textbf{Distributed angular momentum.} The edge at $\Omega_{\max}$ and its tail $t^{-2}$ are
specific to $\delta(L-L_0)$; with a distribution in $L$, $\kappa(L)$ varies and blurs them.
\item \textbf{The regime of comparable masses} ($a_0\gtrsim0.3$), a legitimate but different
physical problem.
\end{itemize}

\section{Tools}
\label{sec:herramientas}

The changes to the scripts are in the repository and are neutral: with their default
values they reproduce the previous results bit for bit (initial state of the Landau run,
linear run $400\times25$, polytrope initial states and tables of \texttt{eta.py}). The only
output that changed is the $T_{\rm rec}$ column of \texttt{eta.py}: $|d\Omega/dJ|$ was obtained by
differentiating the table $E(J)$ twice, which gave noise of order one between
neighbouring nodes, and it is now the mean slope over the support.

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{equilibrio.py}: the option \texttt{-{}-forma} selects \texttt{gauss}
(\texttt{-{}-sigma}), \texttt{politropo} ($J^m(J_t-J)^k$, with \texttt{-{}-jt}, \texttt{-{}-k} and
\texttt{-{}-m}) or \texttt{maxwell} ($E_\gamma(k,(E_t-E)/T)$, with \texttt{-{}-jt}, \texttt{-{}-k} and
\texttt{-{}-w0}); the other options are \texttt{-{}-jmax}, \texttt{-{}-l0}, \texttt{-{}-gq} and
\texttt{-{}-pert} (\texttt{plana}, \texttt{suave} or \texttt{suave3}). It checks the positivity of $F$
and stores in the equilibrium file the shape, $(J_t,k,m,W_0)$ and the perturbation.
$E_\gamma$ is evaluated with its series of positive terms.
\item \texttt{lineal.py}: the linearized Vlasov--Poisson solver; it takes the shape and
the perturbation from the equilibrium; \texttt{-{}-j1 -{}-sj1 -{}-pb} set the test function
$B(J)=J^{p_b}e^{-(J-j_1)^2/s_{j1}^2}$ ($p_b=2$, the one of the code, by default).
\item \texttt{barrido\_\allowbreak lineal.py}: block~L. For each case it builds the
equilibrium, solves the linear dynamics, fits the pole with matrix pencil in windows
$[a,2a]\,\tau_1$ and gives $\omega_b$. It is validated by reproducing bit for bit the linear
solution of 11\_landau and its pole.
\item \texttt{eta.py}: band, shift, $\eta$, $\tau_1$, $T_{\rm rec}$ and cost of a run, without
simulating.
\item \texttt{lambda\_\allowbreak borde.py}: the loop gain $\lambda(\omega)$, $\lambda_{\rm edge}$ and the frequency of
the discrete mode of each equilibrium (Section~\ref{sec:res_lazo}); with \texttt{-{}-borde}, the
approach to the edge, and with \texttt{-{}-debil}, the sharp edges at weak coupling. All of it
takes \SI{11}{min} and is written to \texttt{exe/\allowbreak eta\_\allowbreak lineal/\allowbreak lambda\_\allowbreak borde.txt}.
\item \texttt{colas\_\allowbreak libres.py}: exact free decay of $h_1$ and $\delta\Phi$ by quadrature,
and $\rho_\Phi$.
\item \texttt{landau\_\allowbreak analisis.py}: $\delta\Phi$ and $h_k$ of the PIC runs in the frame of
the equilibrium.
\end{itemize}

Example of use:
\begin{verbatim}
python3 barrido_lineal.py --casos A4 E1 G1
python3 equilibrio.py --forma maxwell --jt 0.138 --k 2 --w0 3 --a0 0.075 \
        --eps 0.07 --pert suave3 --nrc 400 --npc 25 --salida eta/N2.dat
python3 eta.py --forma maxwell --jt 0.138 --k 2 --w0 3 --a0 0.075
python3 colas_libres.py
\end{verbatim}
