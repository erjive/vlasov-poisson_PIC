\title{Self-gravity versus phase mixing in the fixed-angular-momentum\\
       Vlasov--Poisson model\\[0.3em]
       \large Ten particle-in-cell runs across the linear transition}
\author{Working notes}
\date{25 September 2026; revised 29 September 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We study whether the self-gravity of a small spherical component can prevent phase
mixing from erasing a perturbation. The model is the Vlasov--Poisson system in which
all particles share the same angular momentum $L_0$, embedded in a fixed isochrone
background. Linear theory predicts that a collective mode leaves the band of orbital
frequencies, and stops being damped, when the ratio $\eta$ between the
self-gravitational shift of the frequencies and the width of the band exceeds
$\eta_c\approx1.1$--$1.2$. We test this prediction, and the nonlinear behaviour that
linear theory does not describe, with ten particle-in-cell (PIC) runs of $10^4$
particles on a lowered Maxwellian (Wilson) equilibrium, plus seven unperturbed
reference runs. At low amplitude the PIC runs reproduce linear theory: the frequency
and damping rate of a mode inside the band agree to \SI{1}{\%}, the frequency of
discrete modes to $10^{-4}$, and the persistent response of a sharp vacuum edge is
recovered. Near the transition, with $\nu=\omega_b/\gamma=10$, the evolution departs
from linear theory as O'Neil's criterion predicts: damping stops, an island of trapped
particles forms at the edge of the band, its width and bounce period agree with the
pendulum estimate to \SIrange{10}{20}{\%}, and the mode amplitude oscillates with
that period. With $\nu=3$ the late-time departure is not converged in the number of
particles. A discrete mode lying just below the band loses amplitude at
$\varepsilon=0.1$, which linear theory does not predict; the loss is converged and
vanishes as $\varepsilon$ decreases. With a mass comparable to that of the background
($a_0=0.5$, $\eta=5.1$) the discrete mode is far enough from the band to survive
intact even at $\varepsilon=1$. Finally, the initial condition of earlier runs, a
phase-space blob that is not an equilibrium, stops mixing when its mass exceeds
$\sim0.03$: it becomes self-bound. Each run has phase-space figures in $(r,p_r)$ and
$(Q,J)$ and a video.
\end{abstract}

\section{Theoretical framework}
\label{sec:teoria}

\subsection{The fixed-angular-momentum model}
\label{sec:modelo}

We consider a self-gravitating spherical component of mass $a_0$ moving in a fixed
background, the isochrone potential of unit mass and unit scale,
\[
  \Phi_{\rm iso}(r)=-\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{1+r^2}},
\]
with $G=1$. Masses are measured in units of the background mass. All particles have
the same angular momentum $L_0=2$, so the distribution function is
\[
  f(r,p_r,L,t)=F(r,p_r,t)\,\delta(L-L_0),
\]
and $F$ obeys the one-dimensional Vlasov equation with Hamiltonian
\[
  H=\tfrac12p_r^2+\Phi_{\rm iso}(r)+\Phi_{\rm self}(r,t)+\frac{L_0^2}{2r^2}.
\]
The self-consistent potential $\Phi_{\rm self}$ solves the Poisson equation with the
density
\[
  4\pi r^2\rho(r,t)=8\pi^2L_0\int F(r,p_r,t)\,dp_r ,
\]
where $8\pi^2L_0\,dr\,dp_r$ is the phase-space measure of the reduction. The total mass
is $a_0=8\pi^2L_0\iint F\,dr\,dp_r$. We write $\Phi_{\rm ef}=\Phi_{\rm iso}+\Phi_{\rm
self}+L_0^2/2r^2$ for the effective potential.

\subsection{Angle--action variables}
\label{sec:aa}

Each orbit is a closed curve in $(r,p_r)$. Its action $J=\frac1{2\pi}\oint p_r\,dr$ is
the enclosed area divided by $2\pi$, and the angle $Q$ increases uniformly from $0$ to
$2\pi$ over one radial period. In a fixed potential $\dot J=0$ and $\dot Q=\Omega(J)$,
the radial frequency. For the isochrone alone
\[
  \Omega(J)=(J+c)^{-3},\qquad c=\tfrac12\big(L_0+\sqrt{L_0^2+4}\big)=2.414,
\]
so the frequency decreases with the action and the circular orbit ($J=0$) is the
fastest. The angle is measured from pericentre: $Q=0$ at the minimum radius, $Q=\pi$
at the maximum, and $p_r>0$ in between. Near the circular orbit
$r-r_c\approx-\sqrt{2J/\kappa}\cos Q$ and $p_r\approx\sqrt{2J\kappa}\sin Q$, so $J$
measures the size of the epicycle and $Q$ its phase. With self-gravity the map
$(r,p_r)\mapsto(Q,J)$ is computed numerically in the equilibrium potential
(Section~\ref{sec:construccion}). All $(Q,J)$ coordinates shown in this document use
that fixed map.

\subsection{Equilibria and perturbations}
\label{sec:equilibrio}
\label{sec:dato}

A distribution $F_{\rm eq}(J)$ that depends on the phase-space coordinates only through
the action is stationary. Because $J$ is defined in the total potential, which includes
the potential of $F_{\rm eq}$ itself, the equilibrium must be self-consistent; its
construction is described in Section~\ref{sec:construccion}. The equilibria are lowered Maxwellians in the energy,
\[
  F_{\rm eq}=A\,E_\gamma(g,x),\qquad x=\frac{E_t-E}{T}\ (E<E_t),\qquad
  E_\gamma(g,x)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{x^{g+n}}{\Gamma(g+n+1)} ,
\]
and $F_{\rm eq}=0$ for $E\ge E_t$. Far from the edge $F_{\rm eq}\propto e^{-E/T}$; near
the cut-off energy $E_t$ it vanishes as $(E_t-E)^g$. The Wilson model has $g=2$ and the
King model $g=1$. The edge is the orbit of action $J_t=0.138$, $E_t=E(J_t)$, and the
temperature is $T=(E_t-E_c)/W_0$ with $W_0=3$, where $E_c$ is the energy of the circular
orbit. $F_{\rm eq}$ decreases monotonically with the energy, so it has no unstable radial
modes (Antonov). We chose it because its vacuum edge is the object of the theorems of
Hadžić et al.\ (Section~\ref{sec:borde}).

The equilibrium occupies the actions $0\le J\le J_t$, the support, and its frequencies
fill the band $[\Omega_{\min},\Omega_{\max}]=[\Omega(J_t),\Omega(0)]$ of width $\Delta\Omega$.

The initial conditions are the equilibrium plus a perturbation in angle,
\[
  F=F_{\rm eq}(J)\,\big[1+\varepsilon\,s(J)\cos Q\big],\qquad s=(J/J_t)^{3/2}.
\]
It has zero mean in $Q$, so it changes neither the mass nor the equilibrium. The factor
$s(J)$ makes it smooth at the circular orbit, since $J^{3/2}\cos Q\propto J\,(r-r_c)$.
The term $\cos Q$ adds mass on the pericentre side ($Q\approx0$) and removes it on the
apocentre side: the component breathes radially. Since $s\le1$, the condition
$F\ge0$ requires $\varepsilon\le1$.

\subsection{Phase mixing}
\label{sec:banda}

Without self-gravity each point of $(Q,J)$ rotates at its own
frequency, and the perturbation of Section~\ref{sec:equilibrio} evolves as
\[
  \delta F(Q,J,t)=\varepsilon F_{\rm eq}\,s\cos\big(Q-\Omega(J)t\big).
\]
The initial pattern is sheared: the stripes of $\cos Q$ tilt and become thinner in
$J$. Any linear observable, such as the potential perturbation $\delta\Phi$ or the
harmonic $h_1$ (Section~\ref{sec:medida}), is a superposition of phasors
$e^{-i\Omega(J)t}$ that dephase, and it decays by the Riemann--Lebesgue lemma. This is
phase mixing. There is no dissipation: the information moves to ever finer scales in
$J$. The $k=1$ harmonic dephases completely in
\[
  \tau_1=\frac{2\pi}{\Delta\Omega}.
\]
The late-time form of the decay is set by the edges of the
band: an edge at which the weight vanishes as $|J-J_e|^\alpha$ produces a tail
$\propto t^{-(\alpha+1)}$.

\subsection{Collective modes and the coupling parameter}
\label{sec:modo}
\label{sec:eta}

With self-gravity, $\delta F$ generates a potential $\delta\Phi$ that acts back on the
particles. The linearized equation in angle--action variables is
\[
  \partial_t\delta F+\Omega(J)\,\partial_Q\delta F-F_{\rm eq}'(J)\,\partial_Q\delta\Phi=0 ,
\]
and it admits collective solutions $\propto e^{i(Q-\omega t)}$ with complex frequency
$\omega-i\gamma$. If $\omega$ lies inside the band, resonant particles with
$\Omega(J)=\omega$ exchange energy with the mode and the mode is Landau damped,
$\gamma>0$. If $\omega$ lies outside the band there is no resonance and the mode
oscillates without damping: it is a discrete mode, $\gamma=0$. We measure the position
of the mode relative to the band by $x=(\omega-\Omega_{\min})/\Delta\Omega$, so that
$0<x<1$ inside the band and $x<0$ below it. Gravity is attractive and pushes the mode
down, towards $\Omega_{\min}$, the frequency of the largest orbits.

The strength of the coupling is measured by the self-gravitational shift of the mean
orbital frequency relative to the width of the band,
\[
  \eta=\frac{\delta\Omega}{\Delta\Omega/\bar\Omega},\qquad
  \delta\Omega=\frac{\bar\Omega-\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}}{\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}},
\]
where $\bar\Omega$ is the mass-weighted mean frequency of the self-consistent
equilibrium and $\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}$ is the same average for the same distribution
in the isochrone alone. For $\eta\ll1$ the frequency spread
dominates and the perturbation mixes; for $\eta\gtrsim1$ the coupling can pull a mode
out of the band. The value of $\eta$ at which the mode reaches the edge of the band,
$\eta_c$, depends on the family of equilibria; Section~\ref{sec:prediccion_lineal}
gives it for ours.

\subsection{The vacuum edge}
\label{sec:borde}

The distribution vanishes at the edge of the support, $J=J_t$. Hadžić, Rein, Schrecker
and Straub (Arch.\ Ration.\ Mech.\ Anal.\ \textbf{249}, 45, 2025) study this same
reduction: all particles with the same $|L|$, in the fixed field of a point mass (the
isochrone plays this role here), with small equilibria $F=\varepsilon(E_0-E)^k$. For
the linearized problem they prove a dichotomy in the exponent of the edge, valid for
$\varepsilon$ below a threshold $\varepsilon_0(k)$ that they do not estimate. For $k>1$
(our $g$) perturbations are damped. For $1/2<k\le1$ they are not, because there is a
discrete mode below $\Omega_{\min}$. Only the behaviour at the edge matters; for the
King model ($g=1$) they note that there is no damping.

A heuristic argument explains why. The dispersion relation
contains $\int F_{\rm eq}'/(\Omega-\omega)\,dJ$, and with $F_{\rm eq}'\propto(J_t-J)^{g-1}$
this integral diverges as $\omega\to\Omega_{\min}$ if $g\le1$. An arbitrarily weak
coupling then suffices to bind a state at the edge.

\subsection{Nonlinear evolution}
\label{sec:nolineal}

In a fixed potential the Vlasov equation is exactly linear in $F$ for any amplitude
$\varepsilon$; nonlinearity enters only through $\delta\Phi$. Near a resonance
$\Omega(J_r)=\omega$ a particle moves as in a pendulum, with bounce frequency
\[
  \omega_b=\sqrt{|\Omega'(J_r)|\,|\delta\Phi|}\;\propto\;\sqrt{\varepsilon}.
\]
The wave traps particles on a time $\sim2\pi/\omega_b$ and is damped on a time
$1/\gamma$. Their ratio is O'Neil's parameter,
\[
  \nu=\omega_b/\gamma .
\]
For $\nu\ll1$ the wave is damped before it can trap particles, and linear theory holds
at all times. For $\nu\gtrsim1$ the resonant particles are trapped, damping stops, and
the amplitude oscillates and saturates; linear theory does not give the final state.
Near the transition $\gamma\to0$, so $\nu\to\infty$ for any amplitude: at $\eta=1$,
$\nu\approx11\sqrt\varepsilon$, and $\varepsilon\approx0.008$ already gives $\nu=1$.
Whether the evolution is linear is decided by $\nu$, not by $\varepsilon$.

For a discrete mode ($\eta>\eta_c$) there is no resonance inside the band, and the
nonlinear question is whether the mode survives at finite amplitude. Its frequency
shifts with the amplitude, and a mode just below the edge could enter the band. The
relevant ratio compares the bounce frequency with the distance to the edge,
$\omega_b/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$. A third ratio, $\mu=\omega_b/\Delta\Omega$, compares
trapping with the mixing of the whole band. It becomes the controlling parameter when
the initial condition is far from any equilibrium (Section~\ref{sec:bulto_diseno}).

\paragraph{Dependence on the amplitude.} In the linear regime $\delta\Phi\propto\varepsilon$. At
$t=0$ this is exact, because the perturbation of the weights and the Poisson equation
are both linear in $\varepsilon$. Hence $\omega_b(\varepsilon)=\omega_b(1)\sqrt\varepsilon$,
where $\omega_b(1)$ is the bounce frequency of the same perturbation at $\varepsilon=1$, and
\[
  \nu(\varepsilon)=\nu_1\sqrt\varepsilon,\qquad \nu_1=\frac{\omega_b(1)}{\gamma}.
\]
The factor $\nu_1$ depends on the equilibrium and on the shape of the perturbation, not
on its amplitude. The ratio $\omega_b/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$ and $\mu$ scale in the same way,
so their quotients are fixed by the equilibrium; for instance $\mu/\nu=\gamma/\Delta\Omega$.
Two consequences follow. First, in a given equilibrium the amplitude changes all the
nonlinear parameters together; changing one relative to the others requires another
equilibrium, for instance another width $J_t$, which changes $\gamma$ and $\Delta\Omega$
differently. Second, since $\varepsilon\le1$, $\nu\le\nu_1(\eta)$. Far from the transition
the coupling is weak, $\gamma$ is large and $\nu_1$ is small, so large values of $\nu$ may
be out of reach; near $\eta_c$, $\gamma\to0$ and any $\nu$ can be reached. On the discrete
side the ratio $\omega_b/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$ is bounded by $\omega_b(1)/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$,
which decreases away from the transition, so a mode far below the band cannot be made
fragile with $\varepsilon\le1$.

The evolution is therefore controlled by three time scales: the mixing time $\tau_1$,
the damping time $1/\gamma$, which is infinite for a discrete mode, and the bounce
period $2\pi/\omega_b\propto\varepsilon^{-1/2}$.

\subsection{Predictions}
\label{sec:predicciones}

Three predictions follow from this framework and can be falsified by the runs of
Section~\ref{sec:corridas}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[P1.] Where $\nu\ll1$, the PIC evolution follows the linear theory of the same
equilibrium for any $\varepsilon$.
\item[P2.] Where $\nu\gtrsim1$, the PIC evolution departs from linear theory at
$t\sim2\pi/\omega_b$, and damping stops.
\item[P3.] A discrete mode becomes fragile when $\omega_b/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)\gtrsim1$.
\end{enumerate}
P1 and P2 were formulated in the $\eta$ battery, before the runs. P3 was formulated after
the first two runs with discrete modes (D3 and D4) and tested blind with two later runs
at larger mass (D9 and D10). For the non-equilibrium initial condition of
Section~\ref{sec:bulto_diseno} we expected a transition near $\mu\sim1$.

\section{Numerical methods}
\label{sec:metodos}

\subsection{Self-consistent equilibria}
\label{sec:construccion}

The equilibrium of Section~\ref{sec:equilibrio} is a fixed point: the action is computed
in the total potential $\Phi=\Phi_{\rm iso}+\Phi_{\rm self}$, and $\Phi_{\rm self}$ is the
potential of $F_{\rm eq}(J)$ itself. \texttt{equilibrio.py} finds it by iteration. The inputs are the shape of the distribution ($g$, $W_0$ and
$J_t$), the angular momentum $L_0$ and the mass $a_0$. The calculation uses its own
radial grid, $r\in[0.01,25]$ with spacing $0.01$, ten times finer than the PIC grid.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Start from $\Phi_{\rm self}=0$, the isochrone alone.
\item Compute the angle--action map of the current potential. At fixed $L$ the action
depends only on the energy, so it suffices to tabulate $J(E)$. We take 4000 energies,
from that of the circular orbit, $E_c=\Phi_{\rm ef}(r_c)$, to one whose action slightly
exceeds $J_t$. For each energy the turning points $r_\pm$, where $p_r=0$, are found by
bisection, and
\[
  J(E)=\frac1\pi\int_{r_-}^{r_+}p_r\,dr,\qquad p_r=\sqrt{2\,\big(E-\Phi_{\rm ef}(r)\big)},
\]
is computed with 64-point Gauss--Legendre quadrature. At a turning point
$\Phi_{\rm ef}'(r_\pm)\ne0$, unless the orbit is circular, so $E-\Phi_{\rm ef}$ vanishes linearly
in $r$ and $p_r\propto|r-r_\pm|^{1/2}$. The integrand of $J$ is zero there, but its derivative
is infinite. The map also needs the radial period, $2\pi/\Omega=2\int_{r_-}^{r_+}dr/p_r$, for the
angle $Q$, and the integrand $1/p_r$ of the period is itself infinite at $r_\pm$. A Gauss--Legendre
rule with $n$ nodes integrates polynomials of degree up to $2n-1$ exactly, so it converges
fast only for smooth integrands, and slowly for these two. The substitution
$r=r_m+r_a\sin\theta$, with $\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ and $r_m$, $r_a$ the centre and half-width
of $[r_-,r_+]$, makes both integrands smooth. Since $(r_+-r)(r-r_-)=r_a^2\cos^2\theta$ and the
zeros of $E-\Phi_{\rm ef}$ at $r_\pm$ are simple, $E-\Phi_{\rm ef}=r_a^2\cos^2\theta\,h(\theta)$ with
$h$ smooth and positive. Then $p_r=r_a\cos\theta\sqrt{2h}$, and the integrands of $J$ and of
the period become
\[
  p_r\,\frac{dr}{d\theta}=r_a^2\cos^2\theta\,\sqrt{2h},\qquad
  \frac1{p_r}\,\frac{dr}{d\theta}=\frac1{\sqrt{2h}}.
\]
In the bare isochrone, where both integrals are known in closed form, 64 nodes give $J$ to
$10^{-14}$ and the period to $10^{-11}$. Without the substitution the errors are
$2\times10^{-6}$ and $9\times10^{-3}$, and they decrease only as $n^{-3}$ and $n^{-1}$. Inverting
the table gives $E(J)$, and from it $\Omega(J)=dE/dJ$.
\item Evaluate the lowered Maxwellian in this potential. The cut-off energy is that of
the edge orbit, $E_t=E(J_t)$, and the temperature is $T=(E_t-E_c)/W_0$. Both are
recomputed at each iteration, so the support is always $[0,J_t]$ and the depth of the
well is always $W_0$ temperatures.
\item Normalize. The map $(r,p_r)\to(Q,J)$ is canonical, $dr\,dp_r=dQ\,dJ$, and $F_{\rm
eq}$ does not depend on $Q$, so
\[
  a_0=8\pi^2L_0\iint F_{\rm eq}\,dQ\,dJ=16\pi^3L_0\,A\int_0^{J_t}E_\gamma\!\Big(g,\frac{E_t-E(J)}{T}\Big)\,dJ ,
\]
which fixes $A$. The mass is exactly $a_0$ at every iteration; what changes from one
iteration to the next is the shape of $F_{\rm eq}$ in $J$.
\item Compute the density by integrating over the allowed radial velocities at each
radius,
\[
  4\pi r^2\rho(r)=8\pi^2L_0\int F_{\rm eq}\big(E(r,p_r)\big)\,dp_r,\qquad E=\tfrac12p_r^2+\Phi_{\rm ef}(r).
\]
Since $F_{\rm eq}$ is a function of the energy, this step does not need the map.
\item Solve the Poisson equation in spherical symmetry,
$\Phi_{\rm self}(r)=-M(<r)/r-\int_r^\infty4\pi r'\rho(r')\,dr'$.
\item Repeat steps 2 to 6 until $\max_r|\Phi_{\rm self}^{(n+1)}-\Phi_{\rm self}^{(n)}|<10^{-13}$.
No relaxation is used.
\end{enumerate}
The result, $\Phi_{\rm self}(r)$, $\rho(r)$, the table $E(J)$ and the amplitude $A$, is
stored in
\texttt{exe/\allowbreak demo\_\allowbreak eta/\allowbreak ic/\allowbreak <run>\_\allowbreak equilibrio.npz}. The table gives the frequency band and the angle--action map
used by all diagnostics.

\paragraph{Convergence of the iteration.} Steps 2--6 define a map $\mathcal T$ on the
potential, $\Phi_{\rm self}^{(n+1)}=\mathcal T(\Phi_{\rm self}^{(n)})$, and the equilibrium is its fixed
point. By the Banach fixed-point theorem, if $\mathcal T$ is a contraction near the solution,
$\|\mathcal T(\Phi)-\mathcal T(\Phi')\|\le q\,\|\Phi-\Phi'\|$ with $q<1$, the iteration converges
geometrically from any starting point in that neighbourhood, the fixed point is unique
there, and the error of the last iterate is bounded by
$q/(1-q)\,\|\Phi_{\rm self}^{(n)}-\Phi_{\rm self}^{(n-1)}\|$. For small mass the contraction holds:
$\Phi_{\rm self}$ is the potential of a fixed mass $a_0$, and a change of $\Phi$ only changes
how that mass is distributed in $r$, so $\mathcal T$ depends on $\Phi$ at order $a_0$ and
$q=O(a_0)$. The same argument, through the implicit function theorem around the
isochrone alone, gives existence and uniqueness of the equilibrium for small $a_0$. For
finite mass there is no a priori proof, so $q$ is measured: \texttt{equilibrio.py} takes the
median ratio of consecutive changes while both exceed $10^{-10}$, far above round-off, and
reports the bound. Table~\ref{tab:q} gives the values. In each case the ratio is constant
to three digits during the iteration (from $0.174$ to $0.176$ in A4), the signature of a
clean contraction, and for the six equilibria with $g=2$ it follows
$q\approx a_0/(a_0+0.35)$ to within $0.002$. The bound on the error of the final potential is
below $10^{-13}$ in all cases. The script stops with an error if the change is still above
the tolerance after 60 iterations. By the fit this would happen near $a_0\approx0.7$, where
$q\approx0.67$ and about 68 iterations are needed; larger masses would require more
iterations or an accelerated iteration (Anderson mixing).

\paragraph{An independent construction.}
\label{sec:edo}
For the lowered Maxwellian, $F$ depends on the energy only, so the density at radius $r$
depends only on the potential at $r$. With $\psi(r)=(E_t-\Phi_{\rm ef}(r))/T$, integrating over
$|p_r|\le\sqrt{2T\psi}$ gives
\[
  4\pi r^2\rho(r)=C\,E_\gamma\big(g+\tfrac12,\psi(r)\big),\qquad C=8\pi^2L_0A\sqrt{2\pi T},
\]
and $\rho=0$ where $\psi\le0$, because
$\int E_\gamma(g,\psi-p^2/2T)\,dp=\sqrt{2\pi T}\,E_\gamma(g+\tfrac12,\psi)$, term by term in the series
of $E_\gamma$ with the Beta function $B(g+n+1,\tfrac12)$. Poisson's equation then becomes an
ordinary differential equation. With $u=\Phi_{\rm self}-\Phi_{\rm self}(0)$ and
$\tilde E=E_t-\Phi_{\rm self}(0)$,
\[
  \frac{dM}{dr}=C\,E_\gamma\big(g+\tfrac12,\psi\big),\qquad \frac{du}{dr}=\frac{M}{r^2},\qquad
  \psi=\frac{\tilde E-\Phi_{\rm iso}(r)-L_0^2/2r^2-u}{T}.
\]
The centrifugal barrier leaves the centre empty, so $M=u=0$ up to the inner edge of the
support, and outside the support $\Phi_{\rm self}=-M/r$, which fixes $\Phi_{\rm self}(0)$. The system
is integrated only over the support $[r_-,r_+]$, where $\psi>0$ and the right-hand side is
smooth, with the eighth-order Dormand--Prince method (relative tolerance $10^{-12}$). The
three numbers $(\tilde E,T,C)$ are fixed by three conditions: the total mass,
$M(r_+)=a_0$; the depth, $\psi(r_c)=W_0$ at the circular orbit (the minimum of $\Phi_{\rm ef}$),
which is $T=(E_t-E_c)/W_0$; and the edge, $J(E_t)=\frac1\pi\int_{r_-}^{r_+}\sqrt{2T\psi}\,dr=J_t$,
since the turning points of the edge orbit are the edges of the support. They are found
with a Powell hybrid root finder (\texttt{scipy.optimize.root}), starting from the isochrone
alone, with continuation in the mass if it fails. Each equilibrium takes about
\SI{0.5}{s}. This construction has no iteration on a field: its error is set by the
integrator tolerance, and existence and uniqueness reduce to a root problem in three
variables. It applies only to distributions that depend on $E$, such as the Wilson and
King models; the Gaussian and the hollow family of the $\eta$ battery depend on $J$, which
is not local in the potential. It is implemented in \texttt{equilibrio\_\allowbreak edo.py}
and selected with \texttt{equilibrio.py -{}-metodo edo}. In the limit $a_0=10^{-9}$ it
reproduces the isochrone values of $E_t$ and $E_c$ to $1.4$ and $1.6\times10^{-10}$, the
self-potential of that mass ($\approx-a_0/r_c$), and $T$ to $7\times10^{-12}$.

\paragraph{Accuracy of the two constructions.} Table~\ref{tab:edo} compares the two for
the seven equilibria (\texttt{comparar\_\allowbreak equilibrios.py}). They agree to
$1$--$2\times10^{-6}$ in $\Phi_{\rm self}$, relative to its maximum, and to $10^{-5}$ or better in
the density and the parameters. The solution of the differential equation is the
reference: it changes by $4\times10^{-10}$ when its tolerance is relaxed a hundredfold and
its quadrature nodes are halved. The difference is the discretization error of the
iteration. It is dominated by the radial grid: with twice as many energies and momenta it
is unchanged ($1.1\times10^{-6}$ in A4), and with radial spacings $h=0.01$, $0.005$ and $0.0025$
it decreases as $h^2$, to $1.1\times10^{-6}$, $2.8\times10^{-7}$ and $6.5\times10^{-8}$, at the same
$q=0.175$. The mass of the iteration, integrated on its grid, differs from $a_0$ by at most
$1.9\times10^{-7}$ (M5). Both constructions converge to the same solution, and the
remaining error, $\sim10^{-6}$, is far below that of the PIC discretization
(Section~\ref{sec:estacionario}).

\paragraph{Stationarity in the PIC code.} The final test of an equilibrium is to evolve it
with the code at $\varepsilon=0$. The equilibria built by the two methods evolve in the same
way, to better than \SI{1}{\%}, and the departures from stationarity are set by the PIC
discretization, not by the construction (Section~\ref{sec:estacionario}).

\subsection{Particle loading}
\label{sec:carga}

The particles sit at the nodes of a regular grid in the angle--action variables of the
converged equilibrium, $J_i=(i-\tfrac12)J_t/N_{rc}$ and $Q_j=(j-\tfrac12)2\pi/N_{pc}$, with
$N_{rc}=400$ and $N_{pc}=25$, so $N=10^4$. For each node the map is inverted. With
$E=E(J_i)$ and its turning points, bisection finds the point of the orbit at which the
fraction of the radial period elapsed since pericentre is $Q_j/2\pi$. This gives $r$,
and $p_r=\pm\sqrt{2(E-\Phi_{\rm ef}(r))}$, with the positive sign for $Q_j\le\pi$. The
forward map applied to the resulting $(r,p_r)$ returns the nodes with errors below
$10^{-8}$ in $J$ and $5\times10^{-7}$ in $Q$. The weight of each particle is
$F_{\rm eq}(J_i)\,[1+\varepsilon\,s(J_i)\cos Q_j]$. When it reads the file, the code
multiplies all weights by a common constant so that the total mass is $a_0$; since all
grid cells have the same phase-space volume, this only fixes the normalization. The
perturbation changes neither the mass nor the mean profile in $J$, because
$\sum_j\cos Q_j=0$ exactly on a regular grid. This is a quiet start: the perturbation is
carried by the weights, not by the positions, and there is no sampling noise. The
reference runs (Z) use the same nodes with $\varepsilon=0$.

The grid must be built in the self-consistent potential. The code's own \texttt{aa\_quad}
state builds it in the variables of the bare isochrone, and then, once the mass is no
longer negligible, the initial condition is not stationary: the particles are not
where the actual potential would keep them. With the present procedure the action of
each particle in the unperturbed runs changes by at most $1.2\times10^{-4}J_t$ (A1) to
$9.5\times10^{-3}J_t$ (M5, the most massive case), less than \SI{1}{\%} of $J_t$
(Section~\ref{sec:estacionario}).

\subsection{PIC scheme}
\label{sec:pic}

The PIC code (\texttt{VP\_PIC}, Fortran with OpenMP) represents the distribution by $N$
particles. Each particle is a spherical shell with a fixed mass (its weight), a radius
$r_i$ and a radial momentum $p_i$. The radial grid is uniform and staggered,
$r_j=(j-\tfrac12)\Delta r$ for $j=1,\dots,N_r$, with $\Delta r=0.1$ and $r\in[0,20]$
($N_r=200$). The self-gravity is computed in four steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Deposition. The mass of each particle is assigned to the neighbouring grid
points with linear B-spline (cloud-in-cell) weights and divided by the cell volume.
\item Density. Between grid points the density is taken to be linear, with values
rescaled so that the field sees exactly the deposited mass.
\item Field. The enclosed mass and the potential follow from $dM/dr=4\pi r^2\rho$ and
$d\Phi_{\rm self}/dr=M/r^2$, integrated in closed form over each cell. The density is
even at the origin. The additive constant is fixed by $\Phi_{\rm self}+r\,d\Phi_{\rm
self}/dr=0$ at the outer grid point, which matches the exterior solution $-M/r$.
\item Interpolation. The potential and the force are interpolated back to the
particles with the same weights. Beyond the last grid point the exterior solution,
$\Phi\propto1/r$ and $F\propto1/r^2$, is used.
\end{enumerate}
The isochrone force and the centrifugal force $L_0^2/r^3$ are evaluated analytically at
each particle. The equations of motion $\dot r=p_r$, $\dot p_r=-\partial_rH$ are
integrated with the fourth-order symplectic scheme of Yoshida (1990): a symmetric
composition of three kick--drift--kick leapfrog sub-steps with weights $w_1$, $w_0$,
$w_1$, where $w_1=1/(2-2^{1/3})$ and $w_0=-2^{1/3}/(2-2^{1/3})$. Each sub-step includes
a Poisson solve. The time step is $\Delta t=C\,\Delta r/p_{\max}=0.05$, with Courant
factor $C=1$ and $p_{\max}=2$. A particle that crosses the origin is reflected
($r\to-r$, $p_r\to-p_r$), but with $L_0=2$ the centrifugal barrier keeps the particles
away from it. Positions, momenta, weights, the grid potential and the total energy
(kinetic, background, centrifugal and self-gravitational) are written to HDF5 every
$200$ steps, that is, every $10$ time units.

\subsection{Linearized solver}
\label{sec:lineal}

The linear reference is computed by \texttt{lineal.py}, which integrates the linearized
equation of Section~\ref{sec:modo} about the same equilibrium, without particles. It
uses a fixed grid of $N_J\times N_Q=1600\times32$ nodes in $(Q,J)$; the radius $r(Q,J)$
of each node is computed once with the inverse equilibrium map. Each time step, of
size $\Delta t=0.5$, is a Strang splitting: half a field kick,
$\delta F\leftarrow\delta F+\tfrac12\Delta t\,F_{\rm eq}'(J)\,\partial_Q\delta\Phi$,
exact free transport in $Q$, which in Fourier space is $\hat f_k\to\hat f_k\,e^{-ik\Omega(J)\Delta t}$, and a second half kick. The derivative $\partial_Q$ is computed by
FFT. The potential is obtained with the Green's function of spherical shells, which is
exact at the nodes,
\[
  \delta\Phi(r_i)=-\frac{M(<r_i)}{r_i}-\sum_{r_j>r_i}\frac{m_j}{r_j},\qquad
  m_j=8\pi^2L_0\,\delta F_j\,\Delta Q\,\Delta J,
\]
with the shell of node $i$ counted with half its mass. The initial condition is the
perturbation of the PIC runs with $\varepsilon=1$. The solver returns
$\delta\Phi_{\rm lin}(r,t)$ and $h_{1,\rm lin}(t)$ up to the final time of the longest
run on each equilibrium.

\subsection{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:medida}

\paragraph{Potential perturbation.} At each snapshot we compute, on the code grid,
\[
  \delta\Phi(r,t)=\Phi(r,t)-\Phi_{\rm iso}(r)-\Phi_{\rm self,eq}(r).
\]
This quantity does not depend on any angle--action map, and it is the one whose decay
the theorems address. The part due to the perturbation is isolated by subtracting the
reference run of the same equilibrium and dividing by $\varepsilon$,
\[
  \delta\Phi_\varepsilon(r,t)=\frac{\delta\Phi(r,t;\varepsilon)-\delta\Phi(r,t;0)}{\varepsilon},
\]
which is independent of $\varepsilon$ in the linear regime. Its size $\|\delta\Phi\|$ is the
rms value over $3\le r\le15$, where the component lives.

\paragraph{Harmonic.} With $(Q_j,J_j)$ from the equilibrium map,
\[
  h_1(t)=\frac{\sum_jf_jB(J_j)\,e^{-iQ_j}}{\sum_jf_jB(J_j)\big|_{t=0}},\qquad B(J)=J^2e^{-(J-0.05)^2/0.05^2},
\]
with the same subtraction of the reference run and division by $\varepsilon$.

\paragraph{Linear reference and nonlinear deviation.} Each PIC snapshot is paired with
the nearest sample of the linear solution $\delta\Phi_{\rm lin}$, $h_{1,\rm lin}$
(Section~\ref{sec:lineal}). The nonlinear deviation is
\[
  \mathcal D(t)=\frac{\|\delta\Phi_\varepsilon-\delta\Phi_{\rm lin}\|}{\|\delta\Phi_{\rm lin}\|}.
\]
In practice we use envelopes (below): $t_{\rm nl}$ is the first time at which the
envelope of $\|\delta\Phi_\varepsilon-\delta\Phi_{\rm lin}\|$ exceeds \SI{10}{\%} of the
envelope of $\|\delta\Phi_{\rm lin}\|$.

\paragraph{Envelopes, ratios and losses.} The envelope of a signal is its maximum over
windows of $\pm50$ time units, slightly more than one radial period; without it the
oscillation of the mode hides the trend. $R$ is the ratio of the PIC and linear
envelopes of $\|\delta\Phi\|$ in a time window; its error is half the difference between
the values in the two halves of the window. The loss of a run between $5$ and
$20\,\tau_1$ is the relative decrease of the PIC envelope over that interval, net of the
decrease of the linear envelope:
$1-[e_{\rm PIC}(20\tau_1)/e_{\rm PIC}(5\tau_1)]/[e_{\rm lin}(20\tau_1)/e_{\rm lin}(5\tau_1)]$.

\paragraph{Pole fits.} The pole $\omega-i\gamma$ of $h_1$ is fitted, in the PIC runs and
in the linear solution alike, with the matrix-pencil method of order $M=3$ in windows of
a few $\tau_1$. Its uncertainty is the largest deviation among the fits with $M=2$ and
$M=3$ on three trims of the window (complete, without the first \SI{10}{\%}, and without
the last \SI{10}{\%}). This bounds the error of the fit, not the numerical error of the
run, which is assessed with the convergence tests (Section~\ref{sec:controles}). We
tested the fit with synthetic signals of known $\gamma$ at $\omega=0.0725$, sampled every
$10$ time units and fitted over $3600\le t\le7200$ ($10$--$20\,\tau_1$ with $\tau_1=360$): the
frequency, sampling and window of the discrete mode of L5. Both for clean signals and
with a \SI{5}{\%} beat and complex noise of amplitude $10^{-3}$, the fit recovers
$\gamma$ to $\sim10^{-6}$ for $0\le\gamma\le10^{-4}$ (\texttt{demo\_eta.py resolucion}).

\paragraph{Smooth and fine parts.} To separate the large-scale response from structure
on the scale of a few grid cells, $\delta\Phi_\varepsilon(r)$ is smoothed with a Gaussian
of width $0.5$ in $r$. The smooth part is compared with the linear solution smoothed in
the same way, and the remainder, relative to the initial linear norm, is the fine part.
A scale-free alternative is the projection of $\delta\Phi_\varepsilon(r)$ on the profile of
the linear solution at $t=5\tau_1$. The noise floor of a run is estimated as the rms
fluctuation of $\delta\Phi$ of its reference run about its time mean, divided by
$\varepsilon$.

\paragraph{Trapped particles.} A particle is counted as trapped in a time window if its
phase relative to the wave, $\psi=Q-\omega t$, librates, that is, varies by less than
$2\pi$, instead of circulating. The amplitude of the wave felt at the resonance is
$A(t)=|\delta\Phi_1(J_r,t)|$, the first Fourier coefficient in $Q$ of $\delta\Phi$ along
the equilibrium orbit of action $J_r$.

\paragraph{Phase-space figures.} Each particle is plotted in $(r,p_r)$ and in $(Q,J)$,
coloured by its weight perturbation $f_j-f_{{\rm eq},j}=\varepsilon F_{\rm eq}s\cos Q_0$:
red where the perturbation adds mass and blue where it removes it. The weights do not
change, so the colour is a label carried by the particle, and its pattern is the fine
structure of $\delta F$. In the Z runs the colour is $\cos Q_0$, a label without dynamical
effect. In $(Q,J)$ each row of fixed action advances with its $\Omega(J)$, so the initial
pattern of columns tilts, and phase mixing appears directly as shear.

\subsection{Linear theory of the equilibrium family}
\label{sec:prediccion_lineal}

All equilibria are Wilson models with $J_t=0.138$, $W_0=3$, $L_0=2$ and $g=2$, except one
King model ($g=1$, G1a) used to test the vacuum edge. In this family $\eta$ grows slightly
more slowly than linearly with the mass: $\eta/a_0$ decreases from $15$ to $12$ between

\subsection{Choice of \texorpdfstring{$\eta$}{eta} and \texorpdfstring{$\nu$}{nu}}
\label{sec:eligenu}

The two parameters depend on different inputs. $\eta$ depends only on the equilibrium,
that is, on the mass and the shape of the distribution. $\nu$ depends on the
equilibrium and on the amplitude of the perturbation. They can therefore be set
independently: first the equilibrium is chosen for its $\eta$, then the amplitude for
the desired $\nu$.

\paragraph{$\eta$ is set by the mass.} With the converged equilibrium, \texttt{eta.py}
computes $\Omega(J)=dE/dJ$ at 4000 points in $[0,J_t]$ and the mass-weighted mean
frequency
\[
  \bar\Omega=\frac{\int_0^{J_t}F_{\rm eq}\,\Omega\,dJ}{\int_0^{J_t}F_{\rm eq}\,dJ} .
\]
It repeats the calculation with $a_0=0$, the same shape in the isochrone alone, to
obtain $\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}$, and forms
\[
  \eta=\frac{(\bar\Omega-\bar\Omega_{\rm iso})/\bar\Omega_{\rm iso}}{(\Omega_{\max}-\Omega_{\min})/\bar\Omega} .
\]
The amplitude $\varepsilon$ does not enter. There is no closed inverse, but $\eta$
increases monotonically with $a_0$ ($\eta/a_0$ decreases from $16$ to $10$ between
$a_0=0.003$ and $0.5$), so a given $\eta$ is obtained by scanning $a_0$ and
interpolating: $a_0=0.075$ gives $\eta=0.996$ (A4). Changing $J_t$, $W_0$ or $g$ at fixed
mass also changes $\eta$.

\paragraph{$\nu$ is set by the amplitude.} \texttt{lineal.py} gives the pole
$\omega-i\gamma$ of the equilibrium. The bounce frequency is estimated from the
potential of the perturbation at $t=0$, which the linear solution with $\varepsilon=1$
provides:
\[
  \omega_b(\varepsilon)=\sqrt{|\Omega'|\,\max_r|\delta\Phi(r,0;\varepsilon)|},\qquad |\Omega'|\approx\frac{\Delta\Omega}{J_t} .
\]
Since $\nu=\nu_1\sqrt\varepsilon$ (Section~\ref{sec:nolineal}), the amplitude that gives a
chosen $\nu$ is $\varepsilon=(\nu/\nu_1)^2$. The runs were set in this way. For D2, in
A3, $\nu_1=1.17$, and $\nu=0.3$ requires $\varepsilon=(0.3/1.17)^2=0.066$ ($0.065$ was used).
For D5 and D6, in A4, $\nu_1=10.9$, and $\nu=3$ and $\nu=10$ require $\varepsilon=0.075$ and
$0.84$. For a discrete mode the ratio $\omega_b/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$ plays the role of $\nu$. The
amplitudes of the $\varepsilon$ scan of D3 were chosen with it: in L5 the factor is
$19$, and $\varepsilon=0.003$, $0.01$, $0.03$ and $0.1$ give $1.0$, $1.9$, $3.3$ and $6.0$. For $\mu$
the factor is $\mu_1=\omega_b(1)/\Delta\Omega$, $0.25$ in A4. Table~\ref{tab:techo} lists these factors
for the whole family.

a factor of about sixty between $\eta=0.1$ and $1$. A scan in $\eta$ at fixed
$\varepsilon$ also changes $\nu$: at $\varepsilon=0.075$, $\nu$ goes from $0.05$ at
$\eta=0.1$ to $3$ at $\eta=1$. Keeping $\nu=1$ requires $\varepsilon=0.73$ at $\eta=0.6$,
$0.098$ at $\eta=0.8$ and $0.0084$ at $\eta=1$.

Since $\mu/\nu=\gamma/\Delta\Omega$ is fixed by the equilibrium (Section~\ref{sec:nolineal}),
in A4, where $\gamma/\Delta\Omega=0.023$, $\mu=0.023\,\nu$ at any amplitude: D5 has $\nu=3$
and $\mu=0.07$, and D6 has $\nu=10$ and $\mu=0.23$.

\paragraph{The ceiling in practice.} Since $\varepsilon\le1$, and \texttt{equilibrio.py}
refuses to build data with $F<0$, part of the $(\eta,\nu)$ plane is inaccessible
(Figure~\ref{fig:mapa}): for $\eta\le0.45$ this perturbation cannot reach $\nu=1$. On the
discrete side the largest ratio, $\omega_b(1)/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$, is $19$ in L5, $1.7$ in
L6 and $0.63$ in M5, so the mode of M5 cannot be made fragile with $\varepsilon\le1$,
which is what D10 shows.
The ceiling can be raised at fixed $\eta$ in three ways: with a perturbation that
produces more $\delta\Phi$ per unit $\varepsilon$, through another radial profile $s(J)$
or angular profile $\chi(Q)$; with a different width $J_t$ at fixed $\eta$, which changes
$\gamma$, $\Delta\Omega$ and $\nu_1$, although $\eta_c$ moves as well; or with a
non-perturbative initial condition, such as the blob of Section~\ref{sec:bulto_diseno}, whose
amplitude is of order one by construction. The blob, however, is not a perturbation
of any equilibrium, and the parameter that orders its evolution is $\mu$.

\paragraph{Accuracy of the design value of $\nu$.} It is an estimate used to order the
runs and choose $\varepsilon$, not a precise threshold. It uses the maximum of
$|\delta\Phi|$ over all radii and the mean slope of $\Omega(J)$. A resonant particle
feels instead the Fourier coefficient of $\delta\Phi$ along its orbit, which is smaller,
and the amplitude decays with time. In D6 the design bounce period was $1700$; with the
amplitude measured at the resonance the pendulum gives $7700$, and the amplitude
oscillates with a period of $\sim9000$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_nolineal}). The design
value of $\nu$ overestimates the effective one by a factor of a few.

\subsection{Runs}
\label{sec:corridas}

Ten runs cover the regimes of Section~\ref{sec:teoria}: both sides of the transition
at low amplitude, the edge of the transition at increasing amplitude, the discrete mode
at large amplitude, the sharp edge, and a mass comparable to that of the background
(Table~\ref{tab:corridas} and Figure~\ref{fig:mapa}). Each equilibrium also has a
reference run with $\varepsilon=0$ (the Z runs), which is subtracted to isolate the
response to the perturbation. There are seventeen simulations in total.

The damped runs in the linear regime last $10$--$20\,\tau_1$, enough to see the tail after
mixing. For D5 and D6 the relevant scale is the bounce period $2\pi/\omega_b$, $5700$ and
$1700$: both runs cover about three bounce periods, the time over which O'Neil's theory
predicts that damping stops and the amplitude oscillates. These periods were estimated
with the initial amplitude. Since the amplitude decays, the actual period of D6 turned
out to be $\sim9000$, and the run D6L, extended to $t=17\,200$, was added to measure it
(Section~\ref{sec:res_nolineal}).

All runs use the PIC scheme of Section~\ref{sec:pic} with $N=400\times25=10^4$ particles,
and were run one after another with four OpenMP threads. The \texttt{.par} files are in
\texttt{reproducir/\allowbreak corridas/\allowbreak 12\_demo\_eta/} and the whole workflow in
\texttt{reproducir/\allowbreak scripts/\allowbreak demo\_eta.py} (Appendix~\ref{sec:reproducir}). The control runs
of Section~\ref{sec:controles} differ from these only in $N$, $\Delta t$, $\varepsilon$ or
the final time.

\subsection{Non-equilibrium initial condition: the blob}
\label{sec:bulto_diseno}

The runs of Section~\ref{sec:corridas} start from an equilibrium plus a perturbation. The initial condition of
the earlier self-gravity runs (\texttt{reproducir/corridas/09\_autogravedad}) is different:
a blob in angle,
\[
  F_0(Q,J)=e^{-\sin^2(Q/2)/s_Q^2}\;J^2e^{-J^2/s_J^2},\qquad s_Q=s_J=0.1,
\]
generated by the code itself (\texttt{state = aa\_quad}, \texttt{dftype = gauss}) on a regular
grid in the angle--action variables of the \emph{bare} isochrone. All the mass is
concentrated in one orbital phase, with a width of $0.2$ rad. In the terms of
Section~\ref{sec:dato} it is a perturbation with $\varepsilon\gg1$ in all harmonics up to
$k\sim1/s_Q\approx10$, and there is no equilibrium of which it is a perturbation. The earlier
runs used $a_0=10^{-4}$--$10^{-2}$.

Without self-gravity the blob is sheared, and its harmonics $h_k$ tend to zero by phase
mixing. With self-gravity its own field could hold it together. The scale that competes
with the shear is the bounce frequency of the particles in the well of the blob itself,
and the relevant ratio is $\mu=\omega_b/\Delta\Omega$ (Section~\ref{sec:nolineal}): for
$\mu\ll1$ the particles dephase before they feel the well, and for $\mu\gtrsim1$ they are
trapped in it. In runs D1--D10, $\mu$ never exceeds $0.37$.

We ran the same initial condition, on the same grid as the earlier runs
($N_{rc}\times N_{pc}=400\times25$, $J\in[0,0.6]$), at five masses, plus one run without
self-gravity (free mixing) and one with four times more resolution in angle
(Table~\ref{tab:bulto}). The runs use $\Delta t=0.05$ and $t_{\rm fin}=10\,000\approx31\,\tau_1$,
where $\tau_1=327$ is that of the band of the bare isochrone for this distribution,
$[0.0513,\,0.0705]$. $\omega_b$ is estimated from the oscillation of the self-potential
during the first radial period, $\omega_b=\sqrt{|\Omega'|\,|\delta\Phi|}$ with
$|\Omega'|\approx\Delta\Omega/\Delta J$. Since the initial condition is not stationary, there is
no fixed equilibrium to serve as a frame. The harmonics $h_k=\sum_jf_je^{-ikQ_j}/\sum_jf_j$
are computed with two maps: that of the instantaneous potential, which describes the
actual orbits, and that of the bare isochrone, which the code and the earlier runs use.
The two maps agree at large mass but not at small mass (Section~\ref{sec:bulto}).

loses \SI{19}{\%} over the same interval, which linear theory does not predict
(Section~\ref{sec:res_discreto}).

\subsection{Trapping near the transition}
\label{sec:res_nolineal}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{serie_nolineal.pdf}
\caption{The same equilibrium, A4 ($\eta=1$), at two amplitudes: D5 ($\varepsilon=0.075$,
$\nu=3$) and D6 ($\varepsilon=0.84$, $\nu=10$). Dashed: linear theory, which is the same for
both after division by $\varepsilon$. Right: envelope ratio, PIC over linear.}
\label{fig:nolineal}
\end{figure}

A4 lies at the edge of the transition: the linear pole is inside the band but very close
to its edge ($x=0.03$), and it is weakly damped, $\gamma=3.7\times10^{-4}$. With
$\varepsilon=0.075$, $\delta F/F_{\rm eq}$ never exceeds \SI{7.5}{\%}, and any criterion based
on $\varepsilon$ would call the problem linear. O'Neil's criterion gives $\nu=3$.
Figure~\ref{fig:nolineal} compares this run, D5, with D6, at $\varepsilon=0.84$ and
$\nu=10$.

D5 follows linear theory for a long time. The pole of $h_1$ agrees up to $20\,\tau_1$
($\gamma=3.8\times10^{-4}$ against $3.7\times10^{-4}$), and the envelope ratio decreases slowly
from 1 to 0.94. Between $20$ and $30\,\tau_1$ the amplitude oscillates about the linear
curve ($R=0.76$), and from $\sim25\,\tau_1$ on it stops decaying in the base run: over
$30$--$44\,\tau_1$ its smooth part is $4.4$ times the linear one. This late plateau is not
converged (Section~\ref{sec:convergencia}). It does not change with $\Delta t/2$, but with four
times more particles the excess drops from $4.4$ to $1.8$ times the linear value and the
fine part drops by a factor of eleven ($2.2\times10^{-2}\to2.0\times10^{-3}$). Much of the
plateau was discreteness noise, which in these runs grows with time, as documented in
\texttt{PREGUNTAS\_ABIERTAS.md}. Two resolutions cannot decide whether a physical excess
remains; that would require $N\times16$. The robust result of D5 is that it follows
linear theory up to $\sim20\,\tau_1$ at all three resolutions.

D6 departs from linear theory at $3\,\tau_1$. It first decays faster ($R=0.81$ over
$5$--$10\,\tau_1$). Then the damping stops ($\gamma=-4\times10^{-5}$ over $5$--$9\,\tau_1$,
against $+3.4\times10^{-4}$ in linear theory) and the amplitude grows again, reaching twice
the linear value at $13\,\tau_1$. The frequency increases by \SI{0.6}{\%} ($0.0709$ against
$0.0704$). All of this is converged: with four times more particles and with $\Delta t/2$,
$R$ is $0.801$ and $0.805$ over $5$--$10\,\tau_1$ and $1.970$ and $1.966$ over
$10$--$13\,\tau_1$, against $0.805$ and $1.966$ in the base run. The island is visible in
$(Q,J)$ (Figures~\ref{fig:islas} and~\ref{fig:fase_D6}): at the end, the rows with
$J\gtrsim0.11$ wind into a cat's eye with an X point.

\paragraph{Comparison with the pendulum.} To test the island quantitatively, the run
D6L repeats D6 up to $t=17\,200$ ($43\,\tau_1$), several bounce periods
(Figure~\ref{fig:isla}; \texttt{demo\_eta.py isla}). The mode has $\omega=0.07106$. Its
resonance is at $J_r=0.124$, where $|\Omega'(J_r)|=0.095$, and the amplitude of the
potential along that orbit, $A=|\delta\Phi_1(J_r)|$, averages $7.1\times10^{-6}$ over
$10$--$43\,\tau_1$. With these numbers, and no fit, the pendulum predicts two quantities.
\begin{itemize}
\item The half-width of the island, $2\sqrt{A/|\Omega'|}=0.0173$. The measured value, the
median over the trapped particles, is $0.0154$ (maximum $0.025$): the agreement is
\SI{10}{\%}. The trapped particles are identified without reference to their $J$, as those
whose phase $\psi=Q-\omega t$ librates. There are 1090 of them, all from the edge
($J_0\ge0.094$), carrying \SI{0.2}{\%} of the mass, because $F_{\rm eq}\to0$ there.
\item The bounce period, $2\pi/\sqrt{A|\Omega'|}\approx7700$. The amplitude of the mode
oscillates with a period of $\sim9000$: $22$--$24\,\tau_1$ between successive minima, at
$6.6$ and $30.2\,\tau_1$, and between maxima, at $18.0$ and $40.1\,\tau_1$. This is
\SIrange{15}{20}{\%} longer than the estimate, as expected, because particles near the
separatrix bounce more slowly than those near the centre of the island. The second
maximum is lower than the first ($0.47$ against $0.56$), so the oscillation is damped.
It is the amplitude oscillation that O'Neil's theory predicts after trapping. With only
two measured periods, this is a test at the \SI{20}{\%} level, not a precise fit.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discrete modes at finite amplitude}
\label{sec:res_discreto}

\paragraph{The loss of D3.} In D3 the mode lies $2.5\times10^{-4}$ below the edge
($x=-0.01$). The run loses \SI{19}{\%} between $5$ and $20\,\tau_1$ (from $0.685$ to
$0.555$), while linear theory loses nothing ($0.687$ and $0.694$). The fit of $h_1$ over
$10$--$20\,\tau_1$ gives $\gamma=(5.2\pm1.0)\times10^{-5}$, against $(-0.4\pm0.3)\times10^{-5}$ in
linear theory, a difference of five times the error. Two independent checks support the
fit. The envelope of the linear solution, without any fit, gives an effective rate
between $-6\times10^{-6}$ and $+2\times10^{-6}$ over $5$--$20\,\tau_1$; a linear $\gamma$ of
$4$--$5\times10^{-5}$ would make it lose \SI{20}{\%}, and it loses nothing. With synthetic
signals of known $\gamma$ at the same frequency, sampling and window, with a
\SI{5}{\%} beat and noise of $10^{-3}$, the fit recovers $\gamma$ to $\sim10^{-6}$ between
$0$ and $10^{-4}$ (Section~\ref{sec:medida}). The loss of D3 is therefore not linear.
Nor is it numerical: with four times more particles, which doubles the resolution in $J$
near the edge, it is \SI{19.5}{\%}, and with $\Delta t/2$ it is \SI{19.8}{\%}.

\paragraph{Mechanism.} What distinguishes D3 is visible in the particles at the edge of
the band, $J\approx J_t$. A discrete mode resonates with no particle of the support, but
its resonance exists just outside it, at the action $J_r$ where the orbital frequency,
continued beyond the edge, equals $\omega$:
\[
  J_r\approx J_t+\frac{\Omega_{\min}-\omega}{|\Omega'|},\qquad |\Omega'|\approx\frac{\Delta\Omega}{J_t}.
\]
In L5 the mode is $2.5\times10^{-4}$ below the edge and $J_r\approx J_t+0.002=0.140$; in L6 it
is $4.3\times10^{-3}$ below, and $J_r\approx0.164$. The equilibrium has no particles there,
but the finite amplitude of the mode pushes the edge particles out of the support
(Figure~\ref{fig:islas}). In D3 the maximum action grows from $0.138$ to $0.145$ in
$5\,\tau_1$ and to $0.151$ at the end, beyond $J_r$, and the maximum change of action grows
without saturating: $0.007$, $0.013$ and $0.020$ at $t=1800$, $3600$ and $7200$. In D4 the
maximum action reaches $0.142$, far from its $J_r$, and the change of action stays at
$0.003$--$0.004$; in the unperturbed runs it is below $10^{-3}$. We interpret this as
follows: \textbf{the finite amplitude populates the resonance of a discrete mode lying
just below the edge}, and the particles that reach it take energy from the mode, whereas
a separated mode does not reach its resonance. In terms of scales, the bounce frequency
of D3, $\omega_b\approx1.5\times10^{-3}$, is six times the distance to the edge, while that of
D4, $2.3\times10^{-3}$, is half of it. For a discrete mode, the ratio that decides linearity
is $\omega_b/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$, not $\nu$.

\paragraph{Amplitude scan.} With the same equilibrium and four amplitudes, the loss
tends to zero as $\varepsilon$ decreases, the signature of a nonlinear effect
(Figure~\ref{fig:eps} and Table~\ref{tab:eps}). For $\varepsilon\le0.01$ the PIC rate agrees
with the linear one within the error; the loss appears at $\varepsilon=0.03$ and grows
rapidly up to $\varepsilon=0.1$ ($\gamma-\gamma_{\rm lin}\propto\varepsilon^{1.7}$ between the two).
The threshold lies where $\omega_b/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$ exceeds $\sim2$, and it coincides
with the edge particles reaching the resonance. With $\varepsilon=0.003$ they reach
$J=0.1393$, below $J_r\approx0.140$; with $\varepsilon=0.01$ they touch it ($0.1410$), and at
larger amplitudes they go beyond it.

\paragraph{Mass comparable to the background: M5.}
\label{sec:res_masa}
In M5 the component has half the mass of the background. It contracts from $r_c=5.7$ to
$4.65$, where the isochrone encloses a mass of $0.64$: the component weighs almost as much
as the background inside it, and its own field dominates the dynamics. Self-gravity
speeds up the orbits by \SI{150}{\%}: the band moves to $[0.134,\,0.184]$ and $\tau_1$
becomes $125$. The linear mode is at $\omega=0.10487$, $0.029$ below the edge, more than
half the width of the band ($x=-0.59$); the linear solution with half the time step gives
the same value ($0.10485$). The perturbation is almost a pure normal mode: in linear
theory the envelope of $\|\delta\Phi\|$ stays above $0.95$ of its initial value over
$40\,\tau_1$, without the mixing loss of D3 and D4.

The PIC runs reproduce it at both amplitudes (Figure~\ref{fig:masa} and
Table~\ref{tab:polos}). The frequency is $0.10482$ with $\varepsilon=0.1$ and $0.10485$ with
$\varepsilon=1$, against $0.10487$ in linear theory ($5\times10^{-4}$ and $2\times10^{-4}$
relative), and the fit finds no damping: $|\gamma|<10^{-6}$ over $10$--$20$ and
$20$--$40\,\tau_1$. Projected on the mode profile, the PIC amplitude agrees with the linear
one to \SI{1}{\%} up to $10\,\tau_1$; afterwards the ratio oscillates between $0.90$ and
$1.08$ ($\varepsilon=0.1$) and between $0.94$ and $1.05$ ($\varepsilon=1$) because of the beat.
The value $t_{\rm nl}=15\,\tau_1$ in Table~\ref{tab:polos} is not a nonlinear effect: the
small frequency difference accumulates a quarter of a radian of phase over $40\,\tau_1$,
and the PIC$-$linear residual grows with it.

The difference from D3 and D7 is the distance to the resonance. For the mode of M5 it
lies at $J_r\approx J_t+0.029/|\Omega'|\approx0.22$, $0.08$ beyond the edge of the support.
With $\varepsilon=0.1$ the edge particles reach $J=0.140$, with a maximum change of action
of $0.003$ that hardly grows after $10\,\tau_1$. With $\varepsilon=1$ they reach $0.157$, with
a change of $0.02$, also saturated after $10\,\tau_1$. In neither case do they approach
$J_r$. The ratio $\omega_b/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$ is $0.2$ with $\varepsilon=0.1$ and $0.63$
with $\varepsilon=1$, below one in both cases. These two runs were the first test of
prediction P3 made before the run, and the mode survives as predicted. With
$\varepsilon=1$ the static second-order part is larger ($7\times10^{-3}$ of the initial value,
against $\sim10^{-3}$ with $\varepsilon=0.1$), of the size expected for an effect
$\propto\varepsilon^2$.

\subsection{The vacuum edge}
\label{sec:res_borde}

D1 and D8 have the same relative mass ($\eta=0.1$) and differ only in the exponent of the
vacuum edge, $g=2$ and $g=1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:borde}). Up to $2\,\tau_1$ they are nearly

There is a transition in the mass, and it is ordered by $\mu$ (Figure~\ref{fig:bulto_series}
and Table~\ref{tab:bulto}).
\begin{itemize}
\item With $a_0=10^{-3}$ ($\mu=0.1$) the blob mixes almost as it does without self-gravity:
$|h_1|$ drops by four orders of magnitude in $10\,\tau_1$.
\item With $a_0=10^{-2}$ ($\mu=0.3$), the largest mass of the earlier runs, it also mixes,
but $|h_1|$ levels off at $3\times10^{-3}$, forty times above free mixing, and a remnant core
stays in $(r,p_r)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:bulto_fase_B2}).
\item With $a_0=0.03$ ($\mu=0.5$) part of the blob mixes and part does not: $|h_1|$ drops to
$0.2$ and stays there, with \SI{40}{\%} of the mass in a core.
\item With $a_0=0.1$ and $0.3$ ($\mu\approx0.9$ and $1.2$) the blob does not disperse. It
collapses in the first radial periods into a dense core with \SI{79}{\%} and \SI{99}{\%} of
the mass, and $|h_1|$ stays at $0.7$--$0.8$ during the $31\,\tau_1$.
\end{itemize}
The transition lies between $\mu\approx0.3$ and $0.5$. Above it, the well of the blob traps
its particles before the shear separates them.

In this radial model a blob in $(r,p_r)$ is a thin spherical shell. Its own potential has
a kink at the radius of the shell, which creates a local well. The particles are trapped
in it, and the shell moves as a single object: in B4 its mean radius oscillates by
$\pm0.8$ around $5.7$, with frequency $0.075$, above the band of the isochrone. In the map
of the instantaneous potential the core appears at $J\approx0$, since it is the nearly
circular orbit of the potential that it creates. Around it remains a halo of particles
stripped in the first radial periods, and these do mix (Figure~\ref{fig:bulto_fase_B4}).
This is the extreme case of the islands of D6 and D7: there the wave trapped a fraction
of the particles, and here it traps almost all of them.

The observation from the earlier runs, that at larger mass a blob attracts the particles
and the $h_k$ stop tending to zero, is correct, and the mechanism is nonlinear:
self-trapping, controlled by $\mu$. It is not the linear discrete mode of D3--D10. For this
distribution $\eta$ is $0.005$, $0.05$, $0.16$, $0.60$ and $2.5$ from B1 to B5, and the
transition occurs between B2 and B3, at $\eta\approx0.05$--$0.16$, far below
$\eta_c\approx1.1$, where linear theory predicts mixing. At the masses of the earlier runs
($a_0\le10^{-2}$), however, the effect is small and must be measured with care.
\begin{itemize}
\item With the map of the bare isochrone, the one the code uses, $|h_1|$ levels off at
$10^{-3}$ ($a_0=10^{-3}$) and $10^{-2}$ ($a_0=10^{-2}$). With the map of the actual potential
it drops to $10^{-4}$ and $3\times10^{-3}$. The difference is a coordinate effect, already
documented in \texttt{PREGUNTAS\_ABIERTAS.md}: measuring orbits that live in another
potential with the isochrone map leaves a plateau that is not physical.
\item In B2 the high harmonics grow at late times ($|h_4|\approx3\times10^{-2}$ over
$20$--$31\,\tau_1$). This is consistent with the growth of discreteness noise that
\texttt{PREGUNTAS\_ABIERTAS.md} measures for $a_0=10^{-2}$ ($\propto t^{1.9}$, smaller for
larger $N_{rc}$), and we do not interpret it as physical.
\end{itemize}

B4q, with four times more particles in angle, gives the same core mass (\SI{79}{\%}) and a
late $|h_1|$ larger by \SI{6}{\%} ($0.85$ against $0.80$): the conclusion does not depend on
the resolution in $Q$, although the exact value does slightly. The resolution in $J$ and
the time step have not been varied. The open points are a finer mass scan between $0.01$
and $0.1$, the dependence of the critical $\mu$ on the angular width $s_Q$ of the blob (a
wider blob has less self-potential per unit mass), and convergence in $N_{rc}$ and
$\Delta t$.

\subsection{Energy conservation}
\label{sec:energia}

$\max_t|E(t)-E(0)|/|E(0)|\le4\times10^{-6}$, with no appreciable drift, and in the 14 control

runs it reaches $1.7\times10^{-4}$ in B5, the most violent collapse, and $4\times10^{-5}$ in B4.

\subsection{Stationarity of the equilibria}
\label{sec:estacionario}

The seven Z runs have the same equilibrium and initial positions as their D runs, without
perturbation. In them the rows of constant $J$ stay horizontal (Appendix~\ref{sec:ref}):
the largest change of action of any particle over the run ranges from $1.2\times10^{-4}J_t$
(Z\_A1) to $9.5\times10^{-3}J_t$ (Z\_M5). To find what sets these changes we use four
measures, computed every $50$ time units: the largest change of action,
$\max_i|J_i(t)-J_i(0)|$; the mean change weighted by mass,
$\langle|\Delta J|\rangle=\sum_iw_i|J_i(t)-J_i(0)|/\sum_iw_i$; the rms of $\delta\Phi$ over
$3\le r\le15$, relative to $\max|\Phi_{\rm self}|$; and the energy error. The actions are
computed with the angle--action map of the equilibrium and are given in units of $J_t$.

\paragraph{The two constructions.} The equilibria of A1, G1a, A4 and M5 were also built with
the differential equation (Section~\ref{sec:edo}), loaded on the same nodes and evolved with
the parameters of the Z runs (runs E, \texttt{equilibrio\_\allowbreak edo\_\allowbreak pic.py}).
Table~\ref{tab:estacionario} compares the two. All the measures agree to better than
\SI{1}{\%}. The difference between the two equilibria, $\sim10^{-6}$, is not visible in the PIC
code, and the departures from stationarity come from the PIC discretization.

\paragraph{The error of the PIC field.} The resolution runs of M5 in
Table~\ref{tab:resolucion} separate two effects. They repeat E\_M5 with four times more
particles, with half the cell ($\Delta r=0.05$ with the same $\Delta t$, so the Courant
factor is $C=2$), with both, and with half the cell and half the time step.

The first effect is deterministic. At $t=0$ the potential that the code computes from the
particles differs from that of the equilibrium by the error of the deposition and of the
field solve. In M5 the rms difference is $8.7\times10^{-5}$. The particles sit on orbits of
the exact potential, so in the potential of the code they are slightly out of equilibrium,
and the system adjusts. After a few radial periods $\delta\Phi$ has a static part, with rms
$1.7\times10^{-4}$, and an oscillation at the frequency of the discrete mode of M5,
$\omega=0.1048$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_masa}). The oscillation has a constant rms amplitude of
$7.7\times10^{-5}$ from $t=500$ to $5000$: the mismatch excites the mode, and the mode does not
damp. These three numbers do not depend on $N$ or $\Delta t$, to \SI{3}{\%}, and they decrease
by a factor of $3.9$ to $4.1$ when $\Delta r$ is halved. They are the $O(\Delta r^2)$ error of
the PIC field. The same happens in the other equilibria with a discrete mode: in Z\_L5 and
Z\_L6, $\delta\Phi$ oscillates at the frequencies of the modes of D3 and D4
(Table~\ref{tab:polos}), with rms amplitudes of $8.9\times10^{-6}$ and $2.3\times10^{-5}$. Without
a discrete mode (A1, A3, A4 and G1a) the oscillation is below $4\times10^{-6}$, and only the
static part remains, $5.5$--$7.0\times10^{-5}$.

The actions respond to the same error. On an orbit of the code, the energy in the potential
of the equilibrium, $E-\delta\Phi(r)$, varies by $\delta\Phi$ along the orbit, and since
$dJ/dE=1/\Omega$ the action measured with the map of the equilibrium varies by about
$|\delta\Phi|/\Omega$. With $\overline{\delta\Phi}$ of Table~\ref{tab:estacionario} and $\Omega$ at
the centre of the band, this estimate is $6.9\times10^{-6}J_t$ in A1, $8.7\times10^{-5}J_t$ in A4
and $8.7\times10^{-4}J_t$ in M5. It is within a factor of $1.5$ of the measured
$\langle|\Delta J|\rangle$, over a range of more than two orders of magnitude. In M5 the
measured value depends little on $N$ ($1.28$ and $1.21\times10^{-3}J_t$ at $\Delta r=0.1$), and
with $\Delta r/2$ and four times more particles it is $3.2\times10^{-4}J_t$, $3.8$ times less. In

\paragraph{Discreteness of the particles.} The second effect is a growth of the spread of
the actions. It is clear with $\Delta r/2$ and $400\times25$ particles (E\_M5dr):
$\langle|\Delta J|\rangle$ grows almost linearly in time, from $8.5\times10^{-4}J_t$ at $t=1000$ to
$4.2\times10^{-3}J_t$ at $t=5000$, and the largest change reaches $4.7\times10^{-2}J_t$. With half the
time step the result is the same (E\_M5drdt), so the time integration is not the cause.
With four times more particles the growth disappears (E\_M5drN). The spread is symmetric:
in E\_M5dr the mean change of action, weighted by mass, is $3.5\times10^{-7}J_t$, $10^{-4}$ of
$\langle|\Delta J|\rangle$, so there is no heating. $\delta\Phi$ is also the same as with four
times more particles. The energy error behaves in the same way: it is $2.3\times10^{-6}$ in E\_M5dr
and E\_M5drdt, and $2.1\times10^{-7}$ with four times more particles. The same growth is present
at $\Delta r=0.1$, but it is much slower. In Z\_A4, $\langle|\Delta J|\rangle$ rises from
$1.0\times10^{-4}J_t$ at $t=1000$ to $2.9\times10^{-4}J_t$ at $t=17200$. With $\Delta t/2$ (Z\_A4dt)
the values are the same to three digits, and with four times more particles (Z\_A4N)
$\langle|\Delta J|\rangle$ is $9.2$ and $8.8\times10^{-5}J_t$ at the same times. L5 behaves in the
same way. As for the fine part of $\delta\Phi$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_pisos}), the growth falls
with $N$ much faster than Monte Carlo noise would, as expected with a quiet start, whose
noise is not Monte Carlo noise. At fixed $N$ a finer cell makes the growth faster, so
refining $\Delta r$ requires more particles.

At the resolution of the demo the error of the PIC field dominates. It is almost the same in
a D run and in its Z run, which have the same particles, so it cancels in
$\delta\Phi_\varepsilon$ up to the floors of Section~\ref{sec:res_pisos}. The growth from
discreteness adds about $2\times10^{-4}J_t$ to $\langle|\Delta J|\rangle$ by $t=17200$ in Z\_A4.
Neither effect depends on how the equilibrium was built.

\subsection{Convergence in \texorpdfstring{$N$}{N} and \texorpdfstring{$\Delta t$}{dt}}
\label{sec:convergencia}

D3, D5 and D6 were repeated with four times more particles ($400\times25\to800\times50$,
which also doubles the resolution in $J$) and with $\Delta t/2$, each with its own
reference run at the same resolution (Figure~\ref{fig:convergencia}).
\begin{itemize}
\item D3 is converged. The loss is \SI{19.8}{\%}, \SI{19.5}{\%} and \SI{19.8}{\%} at the
three resolutions, and the smooth part relative to the linear one over
$10$--$20\,\tau_1$ is $0.93$ in all three.
\item D6 is converged: $R=0.805$, $0.801$ and $0.805$ over $5$--$10\,\tau_1$, and $1.966$,
$1.970$ and $1.966$ over $10$--$13\,\tau_1$. Its fine part does not change with $N$, so it is
structure of the mode, not noise.
\item D5 is converged in $\Delta t$ but not in $N$ after $20\,\tau_1$. Over
$30$--$43.5\,\tau_1$ the smooth part is $4.4$, $1.8$ and $4.4$ times the linear one, and the
fine part is $2.2\times10^{-2}$, $2.0\times10^{-3}$ and $2.2\times10^{-2}$. Up to $20\,\tau_1$

\subsection{Measurement floors}
\label{sec:res_pisos}

Four effects limit how far a decaying signal can be followed.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Discretization.} At the start, PIC and linear theory differ by $\sim10^{-3}$ of
the initial value of $\|\delta\Phi\|$ in all runs. This is the combined error of the two
discretizations.
\item \textbf{Static second-order part.} The PIC$-$linear residual has a part that does not
oscillate and is constant after $\sim3\,\tau_1$: $5.7\times10^{-4}$ of the initial value in D1
and $7\times10^{-4}$ in D8. It is physical. After division by $\varepsilon$ it grows as
$\varepsilon$ (between D6 and D5 the ratio is 10--11 depending on the window, and that of the
amplitudes is 11.2), so it is an effect $\propto\varepsilon^2$: the change of the mean radial
profile ($k=0$) that the perturbation leaves as it mixes. Since it does not oscillate it
can be subtracted, and what remains is $\sim2\times10^{-4}$.
\item \textbf{Subtraction noise.} The Z run is not exactly stationary: its $\delta\Phi$ has a
static part and, in L5, L6 and M5, an oscillation of the discrete mode, both caused by the
$O(\Delta r^2)$ error of the PIC field (Section~\ref{sec:estacionario}), and fluctuations. The
subtraction cancels them almost completely, but the remainder is divided by $\varepsilon$.
With $\varepsilon\approx0.07$ the floor is $\sim2\times10^{-3}$ (D2).
\item \textbf{Fine structure.} The part of $\delta\Phi_\varepsilon(r)$ on the scale of a few grid
cells, which in the linear solution is a fixed fraction of the signal, has a floor in the
PIC runs: $4\times10^{-4}$ in D1 and D8 ($\varepsilon=1$) and $4\times10^{-3}$ in D2
($\varepsilon=0.065$), again $\propto1/\varepsilon$. In D5 it grows with time, up to
$2\times10^{-2}$ at $44\,\tau_1$, and it is noise: with four times more particles it drops by a
factor of eleven. This is much faster than $N^{-1/2}$, because with a quiet start the noise
is not Monte Carlo noise. For long tails it is better to measure the smooth part, or
$h_1$, which does not go through the grid, and to check with larger $N$.
\end{itemize}
Since the second effect grows with $\varepsilon$ and the third decreases, there is an
optimal amplitude for following long tails, of order $\varepsilon\approx0.3$--$0.5$, with a
floor of about $5\times10^{-4}$. This is an estimate from these runs, not a measurement.

\paragraph{Correction to the previous version.} A previous version of the comparison paired each PIC snapshot with a sample of the
linear solution that, because of rounding accumulated in the sample times, was in
$\sim$\SI{60}{\%} of the cases the next one, two time units later. This does not affect the
envelopes, the ratios $R$ or the PIC poles, but it does affect the PIC$-$linear residuals.
With the corrected pairing, $t_{\rm nl}$ of D3 changes from $7.4$ to $13.5\,\tau_1$ and that
of D5 from $6.7$ to $11.7\,\tau_1$, D4 never reaches it, and the excess loss of D4 over
linear theory drops from \SI{5}{\%} to \SI{1}{\%}. All numbers in this document use the
corrected pairing.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusiones}

\subsection{Limitations of the fixed-\texorpdfstring{$L$}{L} model}
\label{sec:limites}

All of the above holds for the reduction $f=F(r,p_r)\,\delta(L-L_0)$, which is an extremely
anisotropic model. Of a real spherical system it retains the radial dynamics and the edge
$\Omega_{\min}$ of the band. It loses three things.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{There is no spread in $L$.} In a system with distributed $L$, mixing involves
two frequencies (radial and azimuthal), and the spread in $L$ blurs the edge
$\Omega_{\max}=\kappa$. Here that edge is exact and artificial.
\item \textbf{Only radial perturbations exist} ($\ell=0$). There are no non-spherical modes
and no resonances between the radial and azimuthal frequencies.
\item \textbf{The orbital structure is rigid.} The measure $8\pi^2L_0\,dr\,dp_r$ fixes the
weight of each orbit, and the breathing mode is the only collective degree of freedom.
\end{itemize}
These limitations apply relative to a real spherical system. Relative to the theorem of
Hadžić et al.\ there is no such gap: it is formulated in the same fixed-$|L|$ reduction,
with a point mass instead of the isochrone as background, so the comparison is direct
(Section~\ref{sec:borde}). This work addresses what the theorem leaves open: the mass up
to which damping holds for $g>1$ (the $\varepsilon_0(k)$ of the theorem, estimated here as
$\eta_c\approx1.1$--$1.2$ for $g=2$), what happens above it, and the nonlinear dynamics.

\subsection{Conclusions}

\paragraph{Demonstrated} (with the controls of Section~\ref{sec:controles}):
\begin{itemize}
\item The linear solver has a transition from a damped mode to a discrete mode at
$\eta_c\approx1.1$--$1.2$ for this family.
\item The PIC runs reproduce linear theory where $\nu\ll1$, to $10^{-5}$ in $\omega$ and
\SI{1}{\%} in $\gamma$, and they reproduce the discrete modes over $20$--$40\,\tau_1$.
\item Near $\eta_c$, with $\nu=10$ (D6), the PIC run departs from linear theory: damping
stops and the amplitude oscillates. This is converged in $N$ and $\Delta t$.
\item The discrete mode just below the edge (D3) loses amplitude while linear theory does
not ($\gamma_{\rm PIC}=(5.2\pm1.0)\times10^{-5}$, $|\gamma_{\rm lin}|\lesssim6\times10^{-6}$). The loss is
converged in $N$ and $\Delta t$ and vanishes as $\varepsilon$ decreases, so it is nonlinear.
\item A blob in phase with $a_0\gtrsim0.03$ does not disperse.
\item The three linear regimes are those of the theorem of Hadžić et al., formulated in the
same reduction. With a smooth edge ($g=2$) and small mass the perturbation is damped.
With a King edge ($g=1$) and small mass there is an oscillation just below $\Omega_{\min}$
(D8: $\omega=0.06088\pm0.00005$ against $\Omega_{\min}=0.06101$). With $g=2$, damping ceases
above $\eta_c\approx1.1$--$1.2$, a mass that the theorem does not estimate.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpreted} (supported by the particle data and by quantitative tests at the
\SIrange{10}{20}{\%} level):
\begin{itemize}
\item The saturation of D6 is trapping of the O'Neil type: the half-width of the island
agrees with the pendulum to \SI{10}{\%}, and the amplitude oscillates with its bounce period
to \SI{20}{\%}, with only two measured periods.
\item The loss of D3 is caused by the finite amplitude pushing edge particles to the
resonance of the mode, outside the support: the threshold in $\varepsilon$ coincides with
those particles reaching $J_r$.
\item The blob is held together by self-trapping, ordered by $\mu=\omega_b/\Delta\Omega$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Hypotheses} (not tested here):
\begin{itemize}
\item That the late excess of D5 over linear theory is physical. With four times more
particles it drops from $4.4$ to $1.8$ times the linear value, and it is not converged.
\item That $\eta_c$ is universal. Linear theory shows that it depends on the width, the
shape and the edge of the distribution.
\item That the criterion $\omega_b/(\Omega_{\min}-\omega)$ holds in general for discrete modes.
\item That $\eta_c$ for $g=2$ coincides with the $\varepsilon_0(2)$ of the theorem of Hadžić et
al. Here the background is the isochrone, not a point mass; with \texttt{BGtype = sphere}
the code can reproduce their case.
\item That any of this carries over to spherical systems with distributed $L$
(Section~\ref{sec:limites}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Outcome of the predictions.}
\label{sec:predicciones_res}
P1 holds in D1, with $\varepsilon=1$, and in D2. P2 holds in D6, converged, with the
bounce period of the pendulum to \SI{20}{\%}; in D5 the late departure is not converged in
$N$ and counts neither for nor against it. P3 holds blind in D9 and D10, where the ratio
is $0.2$ and $0.63$ and the mode survives, and the $\varepsilon$ scan of D3 confirms it from
the other side: the loss disappears when the ratio falls below $\sim2$. For the blob a
transition near $\mu\sim1$ was expected; it appears between $\mu=0.3$ and $0.5$.

\subsection{Next steps}
\label{sec:siguiente}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{D5 at late times.} With two resolutions it is not known whether a physical
excess over linear theory remains after $20\,\tau_1$. This requires $N\times16$
($1600\times100$, about 12 hours with its reference run) or a diagnostic that does not
depend on the grid.
\item \textbf{The full $(\eta,\nu)$ map} on the damped side, at fixed $\varepsilon$, to see
where the departure starts as a function of $\nu$; and D6 at two or three amplitudes, to
test whether the width of the island scales as $\sqrt\varepsilon$.
\item \textbf{The sharp edge at late times}, with $\varepsilon\approx0.3$--$0.5$, the amplitude
that minimizes the floor, and with the static part subtracted, to follow the response of
D8 beyond $15\,\tau_1$.
\item \textbf{The case of the theorem as stated}, with a point mass as background
(\texttt{BGtype = sphere}), to estimate $\varepsilon_0(k)$ in the same problem as Hadžić et al.
\end{enumerate}
The complete design, with 30 runs and their cost, is in the $\eta$ battery.

\section{Phase-space figures}
\label{sec:fase}

Each figure shows the $10^4$ particles at four times: the initial condition, $\tau_1/2$ (the

end. The top row is $(r,p_r)$ and the bottom row the angle--action variables of the
equilibrium. The colour is the weight perturbation of each particle
(Section~\ref{sec:medida}): red, mass added; blue, mass removed. Since the colour travels
with the particle, the figures show how the flow transports the perturbation.

At $t=0$ the perturbation $\cos Q$ occupies the columns of the grid in $(Q,J)$ and, in
$(r,p_r)$, forms a red crescent on the pericentre side (small $r$) and a blue one on the
apocentre side: the component breathes. Each row of constant $J$ then advances with its

an ever tighter spiral. This is phase mixing. At the end the pattern is finer than the
resolution of the figure and looks like noise, but it is the same perturbation, wound
up. A row of $J$ that stops being horizontal, or particles above the dotted line (the
edge $J_t$ of the support), is not mixing: it is a change of action, which only the
self-consistent field produces. This is what distinguishes D3, D6 and D7.

\section{Videos}
\label{sec:videos}

There is one video per simulation, twenty-four in total (the seven of the blob are named
\texttt{bulto\_B*.mp4}), in
\texttt{reproducir/\allowbreak videos/\allowbreak demo\_\allowbreak eta/\allowbreak <run>.mp4}. They are not under version control and are
regenerated with \texttt{demo\_eta.py videos}; the current files still have Spanish labels.
They are $1280\times720$ pixels, with one frame per snapshot (every 10 time units, about
eight per radial period) at 30 frames per second, and last from 15 s (D2) to 57 s (D5 and
Z\_A4). Each frame has four panels:
\begin{itemize}
\item top left, the particles in $(r,p_r)$, coloured by their weight perturbation as in
Appendix~\ref{sec:fase}, which shows the mixing;
\item top right, the particles in $(Q,J)$, coloured by their \emph{initial} action as in
Figure~\ref{fig:islas}, which shows changes of action: under free mixing each colour stays
in its row, and a row that deforms, or crosses the edge $J_t$ (dotted line), has changed
its action through the self-consistent field;
\item bottom left, the profile $\delta\Phi_\varepsilon(r)$ of the PIC run and of linear theory
at that time, with the vertical axis rescaled to the envelope so that the profile remains
visible as it decays;
\item bottom right, the envelope of $\|\delta\Phi\|$ over the whole run, PIC and linear, with
a line marking the time of the frame.
\end{itemize}
In the videos of the Z runs the colour in $(r,p_r)$ is the label $\cos Q_0$, and the bottom
panels show the $\delta\Phi$ of the unperturbed run relative to the equilibrium potential. Its
static part is the error of the PIC field. In Z\_L5, Z\_L6 and Z\_M5 it also oscillates at the
frequency of the discrete mode, which that error excites (Section~\ref{sec:estacionario}); in
the other Z runs the oscillation is the noise floor.

In D1 and D2 the spiral tightens while the profile of $\delta\Phi$ dies out. In D3 and D4 the
profile still oscillates in the same way at $20\,\tau_1$, although the particles have mixed:
the mode is collective, not a group of particles. D6 and D7 show the formation of the
island in $(Q,J)$. At the end of D5 the PIC curve separates from the linear one and stops
decreasing; with four times more particles the separation is much smaller, so much of it
is discreteness noise (Section~\ref{sec:convergencia}). D10 contrasts with D7: at the same
amplitude, but with the mode far from the band, the rows in $(Q,J)$ move little, no island
forms, and the profile of $\delta\Phi$ follows the linear one.

\section{External review}
\label{sec:revision}

An external review of this work (\texttt{auditoria\_deepseekpro/revision\_cientifica.md})
identified what was missing to turn the demo into a result. The point-by-point response is
in \texttt{auditoria\_deepseekpro/respuesta\_revision\_cientifica.md}. In summary:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Addressed in this version:} the uncertainties of the poles
(Table~\ref{tab:polos}); the accuracy of the fit, tested with synthetic signals; energy
conservation; the smooth/fine split at three scales (Section~\ref{sec:controles}); explicit
predictions; the limitations of the fixed-$L$ model; and conclusions separated into
demonstrated, interpreted and hypotheses.
\item \textbf{Done with new runs:} the $\varepsilon$ scan of D3, the convergence in $N$ and
$\Delta t$ of D3, D5 and D6, and the comparison of the island of D6 with the pendulum (with
D6L, up to $43\,\tau_1$). D3 and D6 are converged; D5 at late times is not.
\item \textbf{Discussed with the data:} whether the loss of D3 depends on the fit (the
linear envelope bounds it without a fit); whether the $Q$ factor in the code's $h_k$
convolves harmonics (the code multiplies each harmonic by a constant); the half-width of
the pendulum island, which is $2\sqrt{A/|\Omega'|}$; whether the fine part should decrease as
$N^{-1/2}$ (with a quiet start the noise is not Monte Carlo noise); and whether the
comparison with Hadžić et al.\ is only an analogy (their theorem is formulated in this same
fixed-$|L|$ reduction, Section~\ref{sec:borde}).
\item \textbf{Pending:} D5 with $N\times16$; the robustness of $\eta_c$ with respect to the edge
(including $g=1/2$), the width and $W_0$ in the linear solver; D8 at the optimal amplitude;
$\eta_c(L_0)$; the quantitative comparison with O'Neil's formulas; and the point-mass case of
the theorem.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproduction}
\label{sec:reproducir}

The whole workflow is in \texttt{reproducir/scripts/demo\_eta.py}. It has six steps, which
are run from that directory and reuse whatever already exists:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lL{9.6cm}r}
\toprule
step & what it does & time\\
\midrule
\texttt{preparar} & the 17 initial states, with \texttt{equilibrio.py}, and their \texttt{.par} files in \texttt{reproducir/corridas/12\_demo\_eta/} & \SI{4}{min}\\
\texttt{lineal} & the 7 linear solutions with \texttt{lineal.py}, up to the longest $t_{\rm fin}$ on each equilibrium & \SI{15}{min}\\
\texttt{correr} & the 17 PIC runs, one after another, with 4 threads & \SI{3.3}{h}\\
\texttt{analizar} & angle--action map of each snapshot (computed once), $h_k$ and $\delta\Phi$; 4 processes & \SI{55}{min}\\
\texttt{figuras} & phase-space figures, time series and \texttt{exe/demo\_eta/resumen.txt} & \SI{2}{min}\\
\texttt{videos} & one video per run, 4 at a time & \SI{50}{min}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The steps \texttt{controles}, \texttt{isla}, \texttt{energia} and \texttt{resolucion} produce the
tables and figures of Section~\ref{sec:controles}. The two constructions of the equilibria
are compared by \texttt{comparar\_\allowbreak equilibrios.py} (Table~\ref{tab:edo}; with
\texttt{-{}-refinar}, also the finer grids of the iteration). Their evolution with the PIC code
is run by \texttt{equilibrio\_\allowbreak edo\_\allowbreak pic.py}, with the steps
\texttt{preparar}, \texttt{correr} (8 runs, \SI{1.7}{h}) and \texttt{analizar}. The last one also
reads the Z runs analysed by \texttt{demo\_eta.py}, and writes the numbers of
Section~\ref{sec:estacionario} to \texttt{exe/\allowbreak demo\_\allowbreak eta/\allowbreak edo/\allowbreak resumen.txt}. The blob has its own
script, \texttt{reproducir/scripts/bulto.py}, with the steps \texttt{preparar}, \texttt{correr}
(7 runs, \SI{1.6}{h}; B4q alone, \SI{47}{min}), \texttt{analizar} (\SI{1.1}{h}: each snapshot is
mapped in the instantaneous potential and in the bare isochrone), \texttt{figuras} and
\texttt{videos}. Its outputs are in \texttt{exe/demo\_eta/bulto/}. All outputs take
\SI{2.6}{GB} in \texttt{exe/demo\_eta/}, outside version control.
